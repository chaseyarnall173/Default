Chapter 2 — Photons: Light as Packets of Energy

  1. An electron absorbs light one photon at a time, and the photon’s energy \(hf\) depends only on the light’s frequency. A red photon (about 1.9 eV) has less energy than sodium’s work function (2.28 eV), so it cannot free an electron, and brighter red light just supplies more photons that each fall short. An electron also can’t pool energy from several red photons, because it loses any extra energy to the metal almost immediately. A violet photon (3.1 eV) exceeds the work function, so even a few violet photons each free an electron at once.

  2. Red: \(E = 1240/650 = 1.91\ \text{eV} = 1.91 \times 1.602\times10^{-19} = 3.06\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}.\) Blue: \(E = 1240/450 = 2.76\ \text{eV} = 4.42\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}.\) Shorter wavelength means more energy per photon.

  3. (a) \(\lambda_0 = 1240/2.1 = 590\ \text{nm}\) (yellow-orange). (b) A 650 nm photon carries \(1240/650 = 1.91\ \text{eV} \lt 2.1\ \text{eV}\), so no electrons are ejected. Equivalently, 650 nm is longer than the 590 nm threshold. (c) A 532 nm photon carries 2.33 eV, which exceeds 2.1 eV, so electrons are ejected with \(KE_\text{max} = 2.33 - 2.1 = 0.23\ \text{eV}.\)

  4. Photon energy \(E = 1240/254 = 4.88\ \text{eV}\). Then \(KE_\text{max} = 4.88 - 4.7 = 0.18\ \text{eV}\), and the stopping voltage is \(V_s = KE_\text{max}/e = 0.18\ \text{V}.\) Copper responds only to ultraviolet, and even this energetic UV gives slow electrons.

  5. (a) Doubling the intensity at fixed frequency doubles the number of photons per second, so the number of electrons ejected per second (the current) doubles. (b) Each electron still absorbs one photon of the same energy \(hf\), so \(KE_\text{max} = hf - \phi\) is unchanged.

  6. Photon energy: \(E = 1240/550 = 2.25\ \text{eV} = 3.61\times10^{-19}\ \text{J}.\) Photon rate: \(\dot N = P/E = 2.5/(3.61\times10^{-19}) = 6.9\times10^{18}\) photons per second, roughly \(7\times10^{18}\). (The 2.5 W figure is an assumed round number; real bulbs vary, but the answer is in the \(10^{18}\)–\(10^{19}\) range.)

  7. One green photon: 2.33 eV. One Wi-Fi photon: \(hf = (4.136\times10^{-15})(2.4\times10^{9}) = 9.9\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV}.\) Ratio: \(2.33/(9.9\times10^{-6}) \approx 2.4\times10^{5}\), so about 240,000 Wi-Fi photons. They can’t do what one green photon does because molecules and electrons absorb photons one at a time. Each Wi-Fi photon has far too little energy to lift an electron to a higher level, so the energy can’t be collected into a single electron. At most, Wi-Fi photons make molecules jiggle slightly, which is heating.

  8. A photon has zero (rest) mass and no charge. It can never be at rest: in vacuum it always travels at \(c\), and it still carries energy \(E = hf\) and momentum \(p = h/\lambda\). It is not made of anything smaller; as far as all experiments show, it is an elementary particle, the smallest unit (quantum) of energy in the electromagnetic field at its frequency.

  9. (a) \(p = h/\lambda = (6.626\times10^{-34})/(532\times10^{-9}) = 1.25\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}.\) (b) \(F = P/c = (5.0\times10^{-3})/(3.00\times10^{8}) = 1.7\times10^{-11}\ \text{N}.\) Check: \(1.3\times10^{16}\) photons/s times \(1.25\times10^{-27}\) kg·m/s each gives the same force. It is far too small to feel, which is why light pressure matters only for things like solar sails.

  10. \(\lambda_\text{max} = 1240/1.1 \approx 1130\ \text{nm}\), in the near infrared. Sunlight with wavelengths longer than about 1130 nm, which is a substantial part of the Sun’s infrared, carries too little energy per photon to free electrons and is not converted. (Photons well above 1.1 eV, like blue light, are also partly wasted: their extra energy becomes heat.)

  11. The photon’s wave, which passes through both slits and interferes with itself, determines the probability of detection: where the wave’s intensity (amplitude squared) is large, the photon is likely to land; where it is zero, it never lands. With only 20 photons you see 20 random samples from that probability pattern, too few to reveal its shape, just as 20 coin flips can’t reliably show a 50/50 split. With thousands of photons the stripes become obvious.

Chapter 3 — The Electromagnetic Spectrum

  1. In common: both are electromagnetic waves, oscillating E and B fields perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel, moving at \(c\) in vacuum and delivered as photons of energy \(hf\). Different: the wavelength (radio waves are longer than about 30 cm; gamma rays are shorter than about 0.01 nm), and therefore the frequency and photon energy (micro-electron-volts versus MeV). That in turn changes how they are made (electrons sloshing in antennas versus nuclei rearranging) and what they do to matter (driving currents and gentle heating versus ionizing atoms).

  2. \(\lambda = c/f = (3.00\times10^8)/(1.00\times10^6) = 300\ \text{m}\), about three football fields. \(E = hf = (4.136\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{s})(1.00\times10^6\ \text{Hz}) = 4.1\times10^{-9}\ \text{eV}.\)

  3. \(f = c/\lambda = (3.00\times10^8)/(940\times10^{-9}) = 3.19\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}\), and \(E = 1240/940 = 1.32\ \text{eV}.\) It is near infrared, just beyond the red end of the visible (700 nm, 1.8 eV).

  4. \(E = 1240/13.5 = 91.9\ \text{eV}\), about 92 eV. That is far above the ~10 eV ionization threshold, so yes, this extreme-UV light is ionizing. It sits near the boundary between UV and soft X-rays, and it is absorbed by air, which is why EUV lithography machines operate in vacuum.

  5. \(\lambda = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}/(1.17\times10^{6}\ \text{eV}) = 1.06\times10^{-3}\ \text{nm} = 1.06\ \text{pm}.\) An atom (~0.1 nm = 100 pm) is about 100 times larger. The wavelength is closer to the size of the nucleus that emitted it.

  6. Absorption happens one photon at a time. Infrared photons carry less than 1.8 eV (typically much less), not enough to break the chemical bonds in DNA and proteins; absorbed IR just makes molecules vibrate faster, which is heat. A very intense heat lamp can still burn you by heating, but that takes a lot of total energy. UV photons carry 3–4 eV or more, enough to break or rearrange bonds directly in skin-cell DNA. That photochemical damage, not heat, is sunburn, and it takes far less total energy.

  7. X-rays are absorbed high in the atmosphere, so essentially none reach the ground; X-ray telescopes must be on satellites. Ground-based telescopes can use the two main atmospheric windows: visible light (with near-infrared windows) and radio waves from about 1 cm to about 30 m.

  8. \(E = 1240/0.1542 = 8.04\times10^{3}\ \text{eV} = 8.04\ \text{keV}.\) To produce diffraction from planes of atoms, the wavelength must be comparable to the plane spacing; Bragg’s law \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\) (Chapter 11) has no solution unless \(\lambda \le 2d\). Atomic plane spacings are a few tenths of a nanometer, similar to 0.154 nm. Green light at 530 nm is about 1800 times longer than the 0.2866 nm lattice parameter, so it cannot diffract from atomic planes and cannot reveal the crystal structure.

  9. Any three of: (1) Sunlight is plentiful in 400–700 nm and the atmosphere is transparent there. (2) Water, which fills the eye and in which eyes first evolved, is transparent in this band but absorbs UV and IR. (3) Photons of 1.8–3.1 eV can reliably flip the shape of the retinal molecule, the first step of vision, without breaking other bonds. (4) UV photons damage molecules, so the cornea and lens filter UV out. (5) IR photons are too weak for a reliable molecular switch; a pigment that sensitive would be triggered by thermal jostling at body temperature, and the body itself glows in the infrared.

  10. Frequency ratio = wavelength ratio \(= 30\ \text{m}/(0.03\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}) = 10^{12}.\) Octaves \(= \log_2(10^{12}) = 12\times3.32 \approx 40.\) Visible light spans a frequency ratio of \(700/400 = 1.75\), which is \(\log_2 1.75 = 0.81\) octave, less than one octave.

  11. The remote’s photons are 940 nm, about 1.32 eV. That is below the energy range your retina’s pigments respond to (roughly 1.8–3.1 eV, 400–700 nm), so you see nothing. It is above silicon’s 1.1 eV band gap, so each photon can free an electron in the camera sensor, and the phone displays that signal as light (often purple-white). Many cameras include an infrared-blocking filter, so the spot may look faint, but it is usually still visible.

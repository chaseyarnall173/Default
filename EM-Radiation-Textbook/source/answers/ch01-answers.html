Chapter 1 — Electromagnetic Waves: Ripples in Fields

  1. The electric and magnetic fields oscillate. At every point along the beam, the strength and direction of \(\vec E\) (and \(\vec B\) with it) swing back and forth at the wave’s frequency. Nothing material travels along the beam: no air, no particles of “stuff.” What travels is the pattern of field oscillation, together with the energy and momentum it carries. If you placed a charge in the beam, you would see it shoved back and forth; that shove is the wave.

  2. A change in a charge’s field spreads outward at speed \(c\). A charge at rest has a static field, and a charge coasting at constant velocity carries a smoothly moving field with it, so no kink ever forms and nothing is radiated. When a charge changes its velocity (speeds up, slows down or reverses), the field near it updates to the new motion while the field farther out still reflects the old motion. The kink connecting the two regions travels outward as radiation. A charge oscillating back and forth is always accelerating, so it launches a continuous train of kinks: an EM wave.

  3. Sound is a pressure wave carried by collisions between molecules, so it needs a material medium. The space between the Sun and Earth is nearly empty, so there is nothing to carry sound. Light is an oscillation of the electric and magnetic fields, which exist in empty space, and each changing field regenerates the other. So light crosses the vacuum and sound does not.

  4. \(\lambda = c/f = (3.00\times10^8\ \text{m/s})/(5.0\times10^9\ \text{Hz}) = 0.060\ \text{m} = 6.0\ \text{cm}.\) That is about half the 12.5 cm wavelength of 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi, since the frequency is about twice as high (\(\lambda \propto 1/f\)).

  5. \(f = c/\lambda = (3.00\times10^8)/(650\times10^{-9}) = 4.62\times10^{14}\ \text{Hz}.\) The period is \(T = 1/f = 2.17\times10^{-15}\ \text{s}\), about 2.2 femtoseconds. Red light has a lower frequency (longer period) than the green light of Worked Example 1.1 because its wavelength is longer.

  6. \(t = d/c = (1.50\times10^{11}\ \text{m})/(3.00\times10^{8}\ \text{m/s}) = 500\ \text{s} \approx 8.3\ \text{minutes}.\) You always see the Sun as it was about 8 minutes ago.

  7. (a) Intensity is proportional to amplitude squared, \(I \propto E_0^2\). Tripling \(E_0\) multiplies \(I\) by \(3^2 = 9\). (b) If \(I\) falls to \(\tfrac14\), then \(E_0^2\) falls to \(\tfrac14\), so \(E_0\) falls to \(\sqrt{1/4} = \tfrac12\): the amplitude halves.

  8. Solve \(I = \tfrac12 c\varepsilon_0 E_0^2\) for \(E_0\): \[ E_0 = \sqrt{\frac{2I}{c\varepsilon_0}} = \sqrt{\frac{2(1361)}{(3.00\times10^8)(8.85\times10^{-12})}}\ \text{V/m} = 1.01\times10^{3}\ \text{V/m} \] Then \(B_0 = E_0/c = (1.01\times10^3)/(3.00\times10^8) = 3.4\times10^{-6}\ \text{T} = 3.4\ \mu\text{T}.\) These are a bit larger than the ground-level values in Worked Example 1.2 because the atmosphere absorbs and scatters part of the sunlight.

  9. The wavelength is \(\lambda = c/f = (3.00\times10^8)/(1.00\times10^8) = 3.00\ \text{m}\). A quarter wavelength is \(3.00/4 = 0.75\ \text{m}\), so the antenna is about 75 cm long. Electrons sloshing along a rod of this length resonate with the wave, which is why it picks up the signal efficiently.

  10. The first polarizer passes half of unpolarized light: \(I_1 = 800/2 = 400\ \text{W/m}^2\), now polarized along the first axis. The second polarizer is at 45° to that polarization, so by Malus’s law \(I_2 = I_1\cos^2 45^\circ = 400 \times 0.50 = 200\ \text{W/m}^2\).

  11. Sunlight reflecting off the lake is mostly polarized horizontally (parallel to the water), because electrons in the water that are shaken parallel to the surface radiate strongly into the reflected direction. Polarized sunglasses have a vertical transmission axis, so they absorb most of that horizontally polarized glare while still passing roughly half of the unpolarized light from everything else, which lets you see into the water. If you tilt your head 90°, the lens axis becomes horizontal. Now it passes the glare and blocks the vertical component instead, so the glare returns.

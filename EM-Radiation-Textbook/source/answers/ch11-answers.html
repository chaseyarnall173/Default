Chapter 11 — X-Rays and Materials Science

  1. The most energetic photon takes all of one electron's kinetic energy: \(E_\text{max} = eV = 70\) keV. Then \(\lambda_\text{min} = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm} / 70{,}000\ \text{eV} = 0.0177\) nm. No photon in the beam can have a shorter wavelength, whatever the current.

  2. The continuum comes from electrons braking in the target, so its upper energy limit is \(eV\): higher voltage moves \(\lambda_\text{min}\) shorter (0.0496 nm → 0.0354 nm) and raises the whole curve. The Kα photon energy is the difference between copper's L and K energy levels, a property of the copper atom, not of the tube. The voltage only has to exceed the 8.98 kV needed to make the K vacancy; above that, more voltage makes Kα brighter but cannot shift it.

  3. \(\mu = (\mu/\rho)\rho = 30.3 \times 11.35 = 344\ \text{cm}^{-1}\). For \(x = 0.025\) cm: \(T = e^{-344 \times 0.025} = e^{-8.6} = 1.8\times10^{-4}\), about 0.02%. That is why a thin, flexible apron works so well at diagnostic energies: photoelectric absorption in high-Z lead is enormous there. Compare the 1 MeV case in Chapter 10, where lead's HVL is 0.86 cm.

  4. Breast tissue consists of fat, glandular tissue, and tiny calcium deposits, all with similar low atomic numbers and densities. Contrast comes from differences in photoelectric absorption, which grows as roughly \(1/E^3\). At low energies those small differences in composition produce noticeable differences in transmission (Worked Example 11.1 shows contrast collapsing from 8.6:1 to 1.2:1 between 30 and 100 keV for bone). Low kV trades a somewhat higher absorbed dose for the contrast needed to see subtle features.

  5. Right: the geometry looks like mirror reflection. The scattered beam leaves the planes at the same angle θ that the incident beam made, and \(\vec d\) is along the plane normal. Wrong: a mirror reflects at every angle, while crystal planes give a strong beam only at the discrete angles where \(2d\sin\theta = n\lambda\). The beam is not a bounce off a surface. It is the constructive interference of weak wavelets scattered by electrons on many thousands of planes deep inside the crystal. Between Bragg angles, the wavelets cancel and the "reflection" is essentially zero.

  6. \(d_{111} = 0.4049/\sqrt3 = 0.2338\) nm; \(\sin\theta = 0.1542/(2 \times 0.2338) = 0.3298\), so \(\theta = 19.26^\circ\) and \(2\theta = 38.5^\circ\). \(d_{200} = 0.4049/2 = 0.2025\) nm; \(\sin\theta = 0.1542/0.4049 = 0.3808\), so \(\theta = 22.39^\circ\) and \(2\theta = 44.8^\circ\). These are the first two peaks used in Worked Example 11.4.

  7. First order: \(\sin\theta = \lambda/(2d_{110}) = 0.1542/0.4054 = 0.3804\), so \(\theta = 22.36^\circ\) and \(2\theta = 44.7^\circ\). Second order: \(\sin\theta = 2\lambda/(2d_{110}) = 0.7608\), so \(\theta = 49.54^\circ\) and \(2\theta = 99.1^\circ\). With \(d_{220} = 0.2866/\sqrt8 = 0.1013\) nm and \(n = 1\): \(\sin\theta = 0.1542/0.2027 = 0.7608\), the same angle. A second-order (110) reflection and a first-order (220) reflection are the same peak, just labeled differently (compare the 99.07° peak in Figure 11.8b).

  8. BCC appears when \(h + k + l\) is even: (110), (200), (211) appear; (100), (111), (210) are absent. Physically, the body-center atoms form a second, identical set of atoms displaced by \((\tfrac12, \tfrac12, \tfrac12)a\). For a given \((hkl)\), that displacement adds a path difference of \((h+k+l)/2\) wavelengths relative to the corner atoms. If \(h+k+l\) is odd, that is a half-wavelength: the two sets, with equal numbers of atoms, scatter exactly out of step and cancel. If it is even, it is a whole number of wavelengths and they reinforce.

  9. \(a = 0.2866\) nm, \(n_v = 2/a^3 = 85.0\ \text{nm}^{-3}\). (110): a rectangle \(a \times a\sqrt2\) contains 4 corners × ¼ + 1 body center = 2 atoms, so \(PD = 2/(\sqrt2 a^2) = \sqrt2/a^2 = 17.2\ \text{nm}^{-2}\). Adjacent (110) planes are \(a/\sqrt2 = 0.2027\) nm apart, and \(n_v d = 85.0 \times 0.2027 = 17.2\); matches. (100): a face has 4 corners × ¼ = 1 atom on \(a^2\), so \(PD = 12.2\ \text{nm}^{-2}\). Adjacent atom-bearing planes are \(a/2 = 0.1433\) nm apart (body centers fill the halfway planes), and \(n_v d = 12.2\); matches. The (110) plane is densest and widest-spaced, so expect slip on {110}, as observed in BCC metals.

  10. Compute \(\sin^2\theta\): 0.1187, 0.2373, 0.3563, 0.4747, 0.5937, 0.7121, 0.8307. Divided by the first: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7. Simple cubic can never give 7, because no integers satisfy \(h^2+k^2+l^2 = 7\). So the pattern must be BCC with N = 2, 4, 6, …, 14: (110), (200), (211), (220), (310), (222), (321). From (110): \(a = \lambda\sqrt2/(2\sin\theta) = 0.1542 \times 1.414/(2 \times 0.3445) = 0.3165\) nm, which matches tungsten (BCC, a = 0.3165 nm; from Callister's R = 0.1371 nm, \(a = 4R/\sqrt3 = 0.3166\) nm).

  11. \(eV = 200\) keV and \(m_ec^2 = 511\) keV, so the correction factor is \(1 + 200/(2 \times 511) = 1.196\). Nonrelativistic: \(\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m_e eV} = 6.626\times10^{-34}/\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(3.204\times10^{-14})} = 2.74\times10^{-12}\) m \(= 0.00274\) nm. Relativistic: divide by \(\sqrt{1.196} = 1.094\) to get \(\lambda = 0.00251\) nm. The nonrelativistic answer is 9% too long, a correction no electron microscopist can ignore.

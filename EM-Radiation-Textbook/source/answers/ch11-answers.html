Chapter 11 — X-Rays: Shadows and Diffraction

  1. The most energetic photon takes all of one electron's kinetic energy: \(E_\text{max} = eV = 70\) keV. Then \(\lambda_\text{min} = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm} / 70{,}000\ \text{eV} = 0.0177\) nm. No photon in the beam can have a shorter wavelength, whatever the current.

  2. The continuum comes from electrons braking in the target, so its upper energy limit is \(eV\): higher voltage moves \(\lambda_\text{min}\) shorter (0.0496 nm → 0.0354 nm) and raises the whole curve. The Kα photon energy is the difference between copper's L and K energy levels, a property of the copper atom, not of the tube. The voltage only has to exceed the 8.98 kV needed to make the K vacancy; above that, more voltage makes Kα brighter but cannot shift it.

  3. \(\mu = (\mu/\rho)\rho = 30.3 \times 11.35 = 344\ \text{cm}^{-1}\). For \(x = 0.025\) cm: \(T = e^{-344 \times 0.025} = e^{-8.6} = 1.8\times10^{-4}\), about 0.02%. That is why a thin, flexible apron works so well at diagnostic energies: photoelectric absorption in high-Z lead is enormous there. Compare the 1 MeV case in Chapter 10, where lead's HVL is 0.86 cm.

  4. Breast tissue consists of fat, glandular tissue, and tiny calcium deposits, all with similar low atomic numbers and densities. Contrast comes from differences in photoelectric absorption, which grows as roughly \(1/E^3\). At low energies those small differences in composition produce noticeable differences in transmission (Worked Example 11.1 shows contrast collapsing from 8.6:1 to 1.2:1 between 30 and 100 keV for bone). Low kV trades a somewhat higher absorbed dose for the contrast needed to see subtle features.

  5. Right: the geometry looks like mirror reflection. The scattered beam leaves the planes at the same angle θ that the incident beam made, and \(\vec d\) is along the plane normal. Wrong: a mirror reflects at every angle, while crystal planes give a strong beam only at the discrete angles where \(2d\sin\theta = n\lambda\). The beam is not a bounce off a surface. It is the constructive interference of weak wavelets scattered by electrons on many thousands of planes deep inside the crystal. Between Bragg angles, the wavelets cancel and the "reflection" is essentially zero.

  6. Bragg with \(n = 1\): \(\sin\theta = \lambda/(2d) = 0.154/(2 \times 0.200) = 0.385\), so \(\theta = 22.6^\circ\). The detector sits at \(2\theta = 45.3^\circ\) from the incoming beam, because it is measured between the incoming and outgoing rays.

  7. \(eV = 200\) keV and \(m_ec^2 = 511\) keV, so the correction factor is \(1 + 200/(2 \times 511) = 1.196\). Nonrelativistic: \(\lambda = h/\sqrt{2m_e eV} = 6.626\times10^{-34}/\sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(3.204\times10^{-14})} = 2.74\times10^{-12}\) m \(= 0.00274\) nm. Relativistic: divide by \(\sqrt{1.196} = 1.094\) to get \(\lambda = 0.00251\) nm. The nonrelativistic answer is 9% too long, a correction no electron microscopist can ignore.

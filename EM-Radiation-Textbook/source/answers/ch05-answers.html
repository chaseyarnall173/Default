Chapter 5 — Absorption and Emission: Electrons Jumping Up and Down

  1. Absorption is all or nothing: the photon must vanish completely and leave the electron on an allowed level. From \(n = 1\) the nearest gaps are 10.20 eV (to \(n = 2\)) and 12.09 eV (to \(n = 3\)). An 11.0 eV photon matches neither, and an isolated atom cannot take 10.2 eV and hand back 0.8 eV, so the photon passes through. A 14.0 eV photon is absorbed: it exceeds the 13.6 eV ionization energy, and above that point the freed electron can have any energy. The electron leaves with \(14.0 - 13.6 = 0.4\) eV of kinetic energy.

  2. \(\Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}\,(1/2^2 - 1/4^2) = 13.6 \times 3/16 = 2.55\ \text{eV}\). \(\lambda = 1240/2.55 = 486\ \text{nm}\): blue-green (cyan), the H-beta line.

  3. \(\Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}\,(1 - 1/4) = 10.2\ \text{eV}\), so \(\lambda = 1240/10.2 = 122\ \text{nm}\) (measured 121.6 nm). That is far ultraviolet, well outside the 400–700 nm your eyes detect. It also never reaches the ground: air (especially oxygen) absorbs such short-wavelength UV high in the atmosphere, so astronomers observe it from space.

  4. Re-emission sends photons in random directions. Of the light the gas removed from the beam, only a tiny fraction happens to be re-emitted along the original line toward you, so the beam you see is depleted at exactly the gas’s wavelengths: dark lines. The missing light has not vanished; it went sideways (and some was lost to collisions as heat). Viewed from the side, the same gas glows at those wavelengths (Figure 5.4C).

  5. Any three of: (1) Direction: reflection follows the law of reflection (one beam at the mirror angle); re-emission goes in random directions. (2) Timing: reflection is effectively instantaneous; re-emission follows absorption by nanoseconds. (3) Phase: reflected light stays in step with the incoming wave, which is why a mirror forms an image; re-emitted photons have random phases. (4) Absorption: reflection involves no absorption at all, while re-emission happens only after a real, resonant absorption. (5) Color: reflected light keeps its wavelength; re-emitted light can be at a longer wavelength (fluorescence).

  6. \(E_\text{UV} = 1240/254 = 4.88\ \text{eV}\); \(E_\text{green} = 1240/545 = 2.28\ \text{eV}\). Lost per photon: \(4.88 - 2.28 = 2.60\ \text{eV}\), or \(2.60/4.88 = 53\%\). Over half of the UV energy becomes heat in the phosphor, a built-in efficiency limit of fluorescent lamps.

  7. \(E = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}/15{,}000\ \text{nm} = 0.083\ \text{eV}\), only about \(0.083/0.026 \approx 3\) times \(k_BT\) at room temperature. Photons this weak are exactly what room-temperature objects (including Earth’s surface) emit in large numbers (Chapter 9), which is why CO₂ intercepts outgoing heat radiation.

  8. A molecule absorbs infrared only if the vibration makes its charge distribution oscillate (a changing dipole), because that oscillating charge is what couples to the wave’s electric field. CO₂’s bending and asymmetric-stretch vibrations shift charge from side to side, and bent, polar H₂O changes its dipole in every vibration. N₂ and O₂ are made of two identical atoms; stretching them keeps the charge perfectly symmetric, so there is no oscillating dipole and no infrared absorption.

  9. \(\lambda \approx 1240/2.26 = 549\ \text{nm}\): green to yellow-green. Each electron must be given at least \(E_g\), so the forward voltage must be at least about 2.3 V. (In practice GaP’s gap is indirect, so pure GaP LEDs are dim; early green indicator LEDs used doped GaP.)

  10. Visible photons carry 1.8–3.1 eV. Sapphire’s gap (about 8.8 eV) is far larger, so no visible photon can lift an electron across it: visible light passes straight through, and the crystal is transparent. Silicon’s gap (1.12 eV) is smaller than every visible photon energy, so all visible light can be absorbed: a wafer is opaque (its gray, mirror-like look is the light it reflects). Silicon also conducts far better: at room temperature thermal energy lifts some electrons across a 1.12 eV gap, but essentially none across 8.8 eV, which is why alumina is used as an electrical insulator.

  11. In thermal equilibrium the lower level always holds more atoms than the upper one (for a 2 eV gap at room temperature, the upper level is essentially empty). A passing photon is then far more likely to be absorbed by a lower-level atom than to stimulate emission from an upper-level atom, so light is absorbed rather than amplified. A population inversion means more atoms in the upper level than the lower. It is created by pumping energy in (a flash lamp, an electric current, or another laser), usually into a level that drops quickly into a long-lived metastable upper level, where atoms pile up faster than they leave.

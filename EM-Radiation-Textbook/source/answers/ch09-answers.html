Chapter 9 — Infrared, Heat, and Thermal Radiation

  1. The hot coffee’s molecules jiggle more vigorously than the room’s, so the coffee has a higher temperature. Because of that temperature difference, energy flows out as heat, and the coffee’s thermal energy (the total random motion of its molecules) decreases until its temperature matches the room. The heat leaves by conduction (through the mug into the desk and the air touching it), convection (warm air and vapor rising away; evaporation carries off a lot of energy too), and radiation (the mug and coffee surface emit infrared, more than they absorb from the cooler room, per Eq. 9.3).

  2. Wien’s law: \(\lambda_\text{peak} = 2898/1500\ \mu\text{m} = 1.93\ \mu\text{m}\), in the infrared. No, most of its energy is not visible. Integrating Planck’s law shows less than 0.1% of a 1500 K blackbody’s output falls between 400 and 700 nm. The steel looks bright yellow-orange (Table 9.1) only because the eye is so sensitive; over 99.9% of what it radiates is infrared, which is why you feel the forge’s heat from across the room.

  3. Power goes as \(T^4\): \((2000/1000)^4 = 16\), so it radiates 16 times as much. The peak moves to half the wavelength: from \(2898/1000 = 2.9\ \mu\text{m}\) to \(2898/2000 = 1.45\ \mu\text{m}\), still infrared, but now much more of the tail spills into the visible, so it glows yellow-white instead of dull red.

  4. Skin \(T = 306\) K; walls \(T_\text{s} = 288\) K. \(P_\text{net} = \varepsilon\sigma A(T^4 - T_\text{s}^4) = (0.98)(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.8)(306^4 - 288^4) = (1.00\times10^{-7})(8.77\times10^{9} - 6.88\times10^{9}) \approx 190\ \text{W}\). That is about twice the 99 W of Worked Example 9.2: the area is 20% larger and, more importantly, the colder walls send back much less radiation, so \(T^4 - T_\text{s}^4\) is about 60% bigger. This is why a room with cold walls feels chilly even when the air is warm.

  5. Window glass strongly absorbs 8–14 µm infrared, because its Si–O bonds vibrate at those frequencies. So the infrared from the street never gets through, and the camera sees only the glass, glowing at its own fairly uniform temperature. Glass is also smooth, so it reflects some of the long-wave infrared hitting it (its emissivity is about 0.9, not 1). You are warmer than the glass, so your reflected infrared stands out as a visible “thermal reflection” of yourself.

  6. The black-anodized section reads hotter, close to the true 80 °C, because its emissivity is high, so the camera is really seeing its own emission. Polished aluminum has \(\varepsilon \approx 0.05\): it emits very little and mostly reflects the cooler surroundings, so it reads near room temperature (or shows reflections of warm objects nearby). To measure it, stick on matte black tape or paint a small spot with a known high emissivity, wait for it to reach the part’s temperature, and read that spot. A contact thermocouple also works.

  7. Examples: (1) A black car gets hot in sunlight, mostly from absorbed visible and near-infrared light, so visible light also becomes heat when absorbed. (2) A microwave oven heats food with 12 cm microwaves, not infrared. (3) A TV remote’s near-infrared beam heats nothing noticeably. Infrared is light; it only becomes heat when something absorbs it. (Also, heat moves by conduction and convection with no infrared involved at all.)

  8. A molecule absorbs infrared only if its vibration makes its charge distribution oscillate (a changing dipole), so the wave’s electric field has something to push on. N2 and O2 are symmetric two-atom molecules: stretching them shifts no charge, so they are transparent to thermal infrared. CO2’s bending and asymmetric-stretch vibrations and H2O’s vibrations and rotations do shift charge, so they absorb. The 15 µm band matters because it sits near the peak of Earth’s ~10 µm glow and just outside the 8–13 µm window, so it intercepts a large share of the outgoing radiation (Figure 9.5).

  9. Power per area: \(\varepsilon\sigma T^4 = (0.85)(5.67\times10^{-8})(280)^4 = (0.85)(5.67\times10^{-8})(6.15\times10^{9}) \approx 296\ \text{W/m}^2\) (the 3 K sky sends back essentially nothing). Area: \(A = 3000/296 \approx\) 10 m². A cooler radiator needs a lot more area, because power drops as \(T^4\).

  10. The orange glow is emitted thermal radiation. The bar is hot enough that the short-wavelength tail of its Planck spectrum is bright in the visible, which means roughly 1000–1100 °C (Table 9.1). The blue sheen is thin-film interference in an oxide layer, seen by reflected room light after the chisel cooled (Chapter 8). It indicates the edge was tempered at roughly 300 °C, far below the ~525 °C Draper point, so steel at that temperature gives off no visible glow at all.

  11. Microwave heating needs water molecules that can twist back and forth with the field (dielectric relaxation). In ice they are locked into a crystal and can barely rotate, so ice absorbs microwaves far more weakly than liquid water. Once one spot thaws, that liquid absorbs strongly and heats even faster, so you get boiling edges around a frozen core. Defrost settings pulse the power on and off so that, during the off periods, heat can conduct from the thawed regions into the frozen ones and even things out.

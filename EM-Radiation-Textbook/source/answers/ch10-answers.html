Chapter 10 — Ionizing Radiation, Shielding, and Space

  1. The X-ray tube. Ionization is decided photon by photon. An infrared photon near 1 µm has \(E = 1240/1000 \approx 1.2\) eV, far below the ~10 eV needed, and an electron cannot save up energy from several photons. The heat lamp's 1000 W just means many more 1.2 eV photons, which warm you. A dental X-ray photon carries tens of keV, thousands of times the threshold, so even a weak X-ray beam ionizes.

  2. \(E = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm} / 254\ \text{nm} = 4.9\) eV. That is below 10 eV, so UV-C at 254 nm is not ionizing. It still damages DNA, because DNA bases absorb very strongly near 260 nm and the absorbed energy rearranges bonds (thymine dimers, Section 10.3). That photochemistry is exactly why 254 nm lamps kill germs, and why you should never look at one or expose skin to it.

  3. Outside the body, a typical 5 MeV alpha particle stops in a few centimeters of air or in the dead outer layer of skin, so it never reaches living cells. Inhaled (radon, for example), the emitter sits right next to living lung cells and deposits all of its energy in a very short, dense track. It makes clustered DNA damage, which is why alpha radiation has a weighting factor of 20.

  4. In an elastic collision, the most energy a moving particle can hand to a target at rest is a fraction \(4A/(1+A)^2\) of its kinetic energy, where \(A\) is the target-to-projectile mass ratio. For hydrogen (\(A \approx 1\)) this is \(4/4 = 100\%\): a head-on hit can stop the neutron dead. For lead (\(A \approx 207\)) it is \(4(207)/208^2 = 1.9\%\), so the neutron keeps at least 98% of its energy each bounce. Hundreds of bounces off lead do what a few bounces off hydrogen do.

  5. Gamma: \(H = w_R D = 1 \times 2.0\ \text{mGy} = 2.0\) mSv. Alpha: \(H = 20 \times 0.10\ \text{mGy} = 2.0\) mSv. One-twentieth the absorbed energy from alpha particles gives the same biological dose, because their dense tracks make harder-to-repair damage.

  6. Inverse-square: \(\dot H_2 = \dot H_1 (r_1/r_2)^2\). At 0.5 m: \(12 \times (1.5/0.5)^2 = 12 \times 9 = 108\ \mu\)Sv/h. At 6.0 m: \(12 \times (1.5/6.0)^2 = 12/16 = 0.75\ \mu\)Sv/h. Tripling your closeness multiplies the rate by 9; quadrupling the distance divides it by 16.

  7. \(\mu = (\mu/\rho)\rho = 0.064 \times 2.3 = 0.147\ \text{cm}^{-1}\). HVL \(= 0.693/0.147 = 4.7\) cm. For \(I/I_0 = 10^{-3}\): \(x = \ln(1000)/\mu = 6.91/0.147 = 47\) cm. (Check: 1/1000 is about 10 halvings, since \(2^{10} = 1024\), and \(10 \times 4.7 = 47\) cm.) This narrow-beam estimate ignores scattered photons, so a real shield would be made somewhat thicker.

  8. \(r = mv/(qB) = (9.109\times10^{-31})(2.0\times10^{7}) / [(1.602\times10^{-19})(3.0\times10^{-5})] = 1.82\times10^{-23}/4.81\times10^{-24} = 3.8\) m. (Its speed is only 7% of c, so the nonrelativistic formula is fine; its kinetic energy is about 1.1 keV.) The 1 MeV proton's radius was 4.8 km, about 1300 times larger, mostly because the proton is 1836 times heavier. Electrons in Earth's field spiral in tight meter-scale helices.

  9. Solar UV: no change. Photons carry no charge, so the magnetic field never affected them; UV reaching the ground is controlled by ozone and the rest of the atmosphere. Cosmic-ray particles: yes. A weaker or shifted field deflects fewer charged particles, so more reach the upper atmosphere, especially at latitudes that used to be well protected. The effect on dose is largest at high altitude (aircraft, the ISS) and smaller at the ground, because the atmosphere's ~10 m-water-equivalent of mass still absorbs most of the energy.

  10. Best to worst per kilogram: polyethylene, then aluminum, then lead. Polyethylene (CH₂) is rich in hydrogen. Hydrogen nuclei cannot fragment, carbon makes relatively few secondaries, and hydrogen gives the most electrons per gram (highest Z/A), so it slows charged particles best per unit mass and also moderates secondary neutrons. Aluminum is light but fragments heavy nuclei and produces secondary neutrons. Lead has the lowest Z/A (about 0.4), so it has the fewest electrons per gram, and its large nuclei shatter incoming heavy ions into showers of secondaries. Its density is no help when the constraint is mass, not thickness.

  11. (a) \(3.1\ \text{mSv} / 0.1\ \text{mSv} = 31\) chest X-rays per year of natural background. (b) \(160 / 0.1 = 1600\) chest X-rays. These comparisons are rough: space radiation includes heavy ions and neutrons whose biology differs from X-rays, which is part of why NASA studies it separately.

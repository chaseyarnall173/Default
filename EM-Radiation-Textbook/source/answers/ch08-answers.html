Chapter 8 — Interference and Diffraction (and Why Size vs. Wavelength Matters)

  1. Near-silence means destructive interference: the two sound waves arrive half a cycle out of step. So the distances from that spot to the two speakers differ by an odd number of half wavelengths, \(\Delta = (m + \tfrac12)\lambda\): \(\lambda/2\), \(3\lambda/2\), and so on. (It is rarely perfectly silent because reflections from the walls add other paths.)

  2. Use Eq. 8.3: \(\Delta y = \lambda L/d = (532\times10^{-9}\,\text{m})(3.0\,\text{m})/(0.25\times10^{-3}\,\text{m}) = 6.4\times10^{-3}\) m. The bright fringes are about 6.4 mm apart. Check: the angle is tiny (\(\lambda/d \approx 0.002\)), so the small-angle form is fine.

  3. First order: \(\sin\theta = \lambda/d\). For 400 nm, \(\sin\theta = 400/740 = 0.541\), so \(\theta = 32.7^\circ\). For 650 nm, \(\sin\theta = 650/740 = 0.878\), so \(\theta = 61.4^\circ\). Second order would need \(\sin\theta = 2\lambda/d\); even for violet, \(2(400)/740 = 1.08 \gt 1\), which no angle satisfies. With the DVD’s finer track spacing, the second-order beams simply don’t exist for visible light, so you only see one steep rainbow.

  4. Diffraction depends on wavelength compared with the opening. Voices have wavelengths from about 0.3 m to 3 m (1000 Hz to 100 Hz), comparable to a 0.9 m doorway, so \(\lambda/a\) is near 1 and sound spreads widely into the next room (wall reflections help too). Light has \(\lambda \approx 5\times10^{-7}\) m, so \(\lambda/a \approx 6\times10^{-7}\). It goes essentially straight through the door, and you only see what is in line with it.

  5. \(K = 100\ \text{eV} = 1.602\times10^{-17}\) J. Then \(p = \sqrt{2mK} = \sqrt{2(9.109\times10^{-31})(1.602\times10^{-17})} = 5.40\times10^{-24}\) kg·m/s, and \(\lambda = h/p = 6.626\times10^{-34}/5.40\times10^{-24} = 1.23\times10^{-10}\) m \(= 0.123\) nm. That is about half the 0.25 nm atomic spacing, so \(\sin\theta = \lambda/d \approx 0.49\) and the first diffracted beam comes out near 29°. Yes, the crystal diffracts it strongly, which is the basis of low-energy electron diffraction used to study surfaces.

  6. \(\theta_\text{min} = 1.22\lambda/D = 1.22(550\times10^{-9})/0.20 = 3.4\times10^{-6}\) rad. On the Moon, \(s = (3.84\times10^{8}\,\text{m})(3.4\times10^{-6}) \approx 1.3\times10^{3}\) m. The telescope can resolve craters about 1.3 km across. In practice, air turbulence often blurs backyard views to about this level or worse.

  7. The Abbe limit is \(d_\text{min} = \lambda/(2\,\text{NA}) = 450/(2\times1.4) \approx 160\) nm, larger than the 100 nm virus, so she cannot resolve its shape. At best a fluorescently labeled virus shows up as a blurry diffraction-limited spot: detected, not imaged. She should use an electron microscope. Electrons accelerated through 100 kV have \(\lambda \approx 0.004\) nm, so diffraction blur is no longer the limit, and resolution reaches the nanometer scale or better.

  8. Rayleigh scattering goes as \(1/\lambda^4\), so the ratio is \((700/450)^4 = (1.556)^4 \approx 5.9\). Blue light is scattered about 6 times more strongly than red, which is why the sky is blue (Chapter 7).

  9. The oven’s wavelength is 12.2 cm, so a 3 cm hole is about \(\lambda/4\), no longer much smaller than the wavelength. The mesh would stop acting like a solid metal sheet, and a significant amount of microwave power could leak out through the holes, which would be unsafe and waste energy. The 1–2 mm holes are roughly \(\lambda/100\), so almost nothing gets through. Visible light passes either way, because both hole sizes are thousands of wavelengths across.

  10. For diffraction you need \(\sin\theta = m\lambda/d \le 1\), with angles big enough to measure. Visible light (\(\lambda \approx 500\) nm) on planes 0.2 nm apart would need \(\sin\theta = 2500\), which is impossible, so there are no diffracted beams at all and the crystal acts like a uniform medium. X-rays with \(\lambda = 0.154\) nm give \(\lambda/d \approx 0.77\), a large, easily measured angle (about 50° in this simple model; Bragg’s law in Chapter 11 handles the reflection geometry). The wavelength must be comparable to the spacing you want to measure.

  11. \(d/\lambda = 10/25.984 \approx 0.38\). On Figure 8.6 that sits at the lower edge of the Mie regime (the curve gives \(Q \approx 0.2\) for a water sphere), not deep in the weak Rayleigh region, so the cell interacts substantially with this light. More importantly, emission and absorption are set by resonance, not size: molecules a fraction of a nanometer across emit and absorb infrared wavelengths of many micrometers. What would be strange is a cell staying perfectly black at wavelengths far larger than itself.

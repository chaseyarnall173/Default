Chapter 4 — Inside the Atom: Scale, Orbitals, and Quantized Energy

  1. An atom has no surface. Its electron cloud thins out smoothly with distance and never reaches exactly zero, so any “diameter” depends on where you choose to draw the line (for example, the sphere enclosing 90% of the probability). In practice, radii are measured as half the equilibrium distance between neighboring nuclei, and that distance depends on the bonding environment. The same element therefore has a metallic radius, a covalent radius and (as an ion) an ionic radius, all slightly different.

  2. Diameter \(d = 2(0.143\ \text{nm}) = 0.286\ \text{nm}\). Number of atoms in 1 mm: \(N = (1\times10^{-3}\ \text{m})/(0.286\times10^{-9}\ \text{m}) = 3.5\times10^{6}\). About 3.5 million aluminum atoms span a millimeter.

  3. \(R = 1.2\ \text{fm} \times 56^{1/3} = 1.2 \times 3.83 = 4.59\ \text{fm}\), so the nuclear diameter is about 9.2 fm. Ratio of diameters: \(0.248\times10^{-9}/9.18\times10^{-15} \approx 27{,}000\). Scaled up: \(1.5\ \text{cm} \times 27{,}000 \approx 405\ \text{m}\). The iron atom would be about 400 m across, nearly four football fields.

  4. Any two of: (1) Stability: a charge moving in a circle is accelerating and must radiate (Chapter 1), so a planetary electron would lose energy and spiral into the nucleus almost instantly, yet atoms are stable. (2) Discrete energies: a planet can orbit at any distance with any energy, but atoms absorb and emit only specific photon energies, revealing a ladder of allowed levels. (3) Shape: hydrogen’s ground state is spherically symmetric with zero orbital angular momentum, not a flat circular track; quantum mechanics gives a probability cloud, not a trajectory.

  5. A string fixed at both ends can only vibrate in patterns where a whole number of half-wavelengths fits between the ends, so only certain frequencies (notes) are possible. A bound electron is a wave confined by the electric attraction of the nucleus, so it too can exist only in standing-wave patterns that “fit,” and each pattern has a definite energy. A free electron is not confined; any wavelength is allowed, so its energy can take any value (the continuum above 0 eV in Figure 4.5).

  6. \(\lambda = h/(mv) = (6.626\times10^{-34})/[(9.11\times10^{-31})(1.09\times10^{6})] = 6.67\times10^{-10}\ \text{m} = 0.667\ \text{nm}\). Circumference: \(2\pi(0.212\ \text{nm}) = 1.33\ \text{nm}\). Ratio: \(1.33/0.667 = 2.0\). Exactly two wavelengths fit, as Equation 4.2 requires for \(n = 2\).

  7. \(E_3 = -13.6/9 = -1.51\ \text{eV}\), so 1.51 eV ionizes it. A 2.0 eV photon has more than enough: it frees the electron, and the extra \(2.0 - 1.51 = 0.49\ \text{eV}\) becomes the electron’s kinetic energy. A ground-state atom needs 13.6 eV, so the same photon cannot ionize it. This is why visible light is “non-ionizing” for ordinary (ground-state) atoms.

  8. Neon (21.6 eV) > magnesium (7.65 eV) > sodium (5.14 eV). Neon’s outer shell (\(n = 2\)) is completely full and held close to the nucleus, so removing an electron is very hard. Sodium has a single 3s electron alone in a new, larger shell, screened from its nucleus by the ten inner electrons, so it leaves easily. Magnesium also has its outer electrons in the 3s shell, but it has one more proton pulling on them than sodium does, so they are held somewhat more tightly, though still far less tightly than neon’s.

  9. When your hand’s atoms approach the table’s atoms, their electron clouds start to overlap. (1) Electric repulsion: the negative clouds repel each other, and each nucleus becomes less shielded from the other nucleus. (2) The Pauli exclusion principle: two electrons cannot share the same quantum state, so squeezing filled clouds together forces electrons into higher-energy states. Together these make the energy climb steeply at separations below \(r_0\) (Figure 4.7). The resulting repulsive force stops your hand when your atoms are still about 0.1–0.3 nm from the table’s.

Prologue

The Whole Story in Ten Minutes

Radio, light, color, heat, sunburn, X-rays: everything in this book is one story told at different wavelengths. Read this first. It is the map, and whenever you feel lost in a detail, come back here and ask: which step of the story am I in?

1

Light is a ripple in electric and magnetic fields

Every electric charge is surrounded by an electric field, an invisible “push-pull” on other charges, and moving charges also make a magnetic field. Shake a charge and the change in its field cannot reach the rest of the universe instantly; it spreads outward at \(c = 3.00\times10^{8}\) m/s. A changing electric field creates a magnetic field, and a changing magnetic field creates an electric field, so once launched the disturbance keeps itself going and needs no medium at all. That self-sustaining ripple is an electromagnetic (EM) wave. Its wavelength \(\lambda\) and frequency \(f\) are tied together by \(c = \lambda f\).

→ CHAPTER 1

2

The wave’s energy arrives in packets called photons

Light delivers its energy in indivisible lumps called photons, each carrying \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\). That gives you two separate dials. Frequency (color) sets the energy of each photon. Amplitude (brightness) sets how many photons arrive per second. The photoelectric effect proves it: dim ultraviolet light knocks electrons out of a metal, while blinding red light cannot, because no single red photon has enough energy. Handy shortcut: \(E\,(\text{eV}) = 1240/\lambda\,(\text{nm})\), so a 500 nm green photon carries about 2.5 eV.

→ CHAPTER 2

3

Radio waves, light and X-rays are the same thing

The electromagnetic spectrum runs from radio waves longer than buildings to gamma rays smaller than atomic nuclei. They all travel at \(c\) and are all made of photons. The only difference is wavelength, which means photon energy, which decides what the light can do to matter (Figure P.1). Visible light, 400 to 700 nm, or 1.8 to 3.1 eV per photon, is a tiny slice in the middle.

→ CHAPTER 3

4

Matter is electrons on an energy ladder

An atom is about 0.1 to 0.3 nm across, and its nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller still. The electrons are spread out in probability clouds, and they can only have certain energies: rungs on a ladder. Molecules add more closely spaced rungs for vibration (infrared-sized steps) and rotation (microwave-sized steps). In a solid, the rungs of trillions of atoms smear together into energy bands separated by band gaps.

→ CHAPTERS 4 AND 5

5

One question decides what light does to matter

When light hits a material, ask one thing: is there an allowed jump whose energy matches the photon’s energy \(hf\)? If yes, the photon can be absorbed, and its energy is then re-emitted, turned into heat, used for chemistry, or (if it is big enough) spent knocking an electron out entirely. If no, the photon is not absorbed. Instead the wave shakes the electrons, and they re-radiate in step with it. That coherent re-radiation produces reflection, transmission, refraction and scattering (Figure P.2). Notice what does not appear in the question: the size of the atom. An atom 5,000 times smaller than a light wave absorbs it happily, because absorption is about matching energy (resonance), not matching size.

→ CHAPTERS 5, 6 AND 8

6

Color is what survives

White light contains every visible wavelength. A material absorbs the wavelengths that match its available jumps, and your eye receives whatever is left over: a leaf absorbs red and blue and returns green. In solids the band gap decides a lot. A gap bigger than 3.1 eV absorbs no visible light (pure diamond and pure sapphire are clear), a gap smaller than 1.8 eV absorbs all of it (silicon looks gray and opaque), and a gap in between absorbs only part of the spectrum and looks colored. The sky is blue for a different reason: tiny air molecules scatter short wavelengths far more strongly than long ones.

→ CHAPTER 7

7

Waves spread out and interfere

Because light is a wave, it bends around obstacles and spreads through openings (diffraction), and overlapping waves reinforce or cancel (interference). This sets a hard limit: you cannot image details much smaller than about half a wavelength, which is why no ordinary microscope will ever show you an atom. Objects much smaller than the wavelength also scatter it only weakly. That is why the metal mesh in a microwave oven door blocks 12 cm microwaves but lets visible light through.

→ CHAPTER 8

8

Everything glows, and warm things glow in infrared

Every object above absolute zero emits thermal radiation with a spectrum set by its temperature. At body temperature (310 K) you glow most strongly near 9 to 10 µm, in the infrared. A stove burner at about 1000 K glows dull red, and the Sun’s 5800 K surface peaks in the visible. Infrared gets called “heat radiation” because everyday warm objects emit mostly IR, and because IR photons match molecular vibrations, so materials soak IR up and turn it straight into random thermal motion. But IR is light, not heat itself, and visible light heats things too.

→ CHAPTER 9

9

Above about 10 eV, light becomes ionizing

Once a single photon carries more energy than an electron’s binding energy (roughly 10 eV, in the far ultraviolet), it can knock that electron off its atom. That breaks chemical bonds and can damage DNA. At everyday intensities, brightness can’t make up for low photon energy: a billion radio photons can’t do what one X-ray photon does. The word “radiation” also covers fast particles such as alpha, beta, neutrons and cosmic rays. Magnetic fields can steer charged particles away (Earth’s magnetosphere does this), but they do nothing to photons. Only matter stops those.

→ CHAPTER 10

10

X-rays fit between the atoms

X-ray wavelengths (about 0.1 nm) match the spacing between planes of atoms in a crystal, so a crystal acts like a three-dimensional diffraction grating. Reflections appear only at angles satisfying Bragg’s law, \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\). This is how we know that copper is FCC and iron is BCC, and how lattice parameters are measured. The Miller-index planes from your materials science course are, quite literally, the mirrors that reflect X-rays.

Key Idea — Three equations carry most of this book

\( c = \lambda f \) (wavelength and frequency are locked together) · \( E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} \) (photon energy grows with frequency) · \( E_\text{photon} = \Delta E_\text{jump} \) (light is absorbed or emitted only when its photon energy matches an allowed jump). Nearly everything else is these three ideas applied to a particular wavelength and a particular material.

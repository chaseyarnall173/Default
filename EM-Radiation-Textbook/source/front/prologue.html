Prologue

The Whole Story in Ten Minutes

Radio, light, color, heat, sunburn, X-rays: one story told at different wavelengths. This is the map. When you feel lost in a detail, come back and ask: which step am I on?

1

Light is a ripple in electric and magnetic fields

Charges are surrounded by an electric field, and moving charges also make a magnetic field. Shake a charge and the change in its field spreads outward at \(c = 3.00\times10^{8}\) m/s. A changing electric field makes a magnetic field and vice versa, so the ripple sustains itself and needs no medium. That ripple is an electromagnetic (EM) wave, with \(c = \lambda f\).

→ CHAPTER 1

2

The wave’s energy arrives in packets called photons

Light delivers energy in lumps called photons, each with \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\). Two separate dials: frequency (color) sets the energy per photon; amplitude (brightness) sets how many photons arrive. Proof: dim UV knocks electrons out of metal, but blinding red light can’t, because no single red photon has enough energy. A 500 nm photon carries \(1240/500 \approx 2.5\) eV.

→ CHAPTER 2

3

Radio waves, light and X-rays are the same thing

From radio waves longer than buildings to gamma rays smaller than nuclei, it’s all the same thing traveling at \(c\). The only difference is wavelength, which sets photon energy, which decides what the light does to matter (Figure P.1). Visible light (400–700 nm, 1.8–3.1 eV) is a tiny slice.

→ CHAPTER 3

4

Matter is electrons on an energy ladder

An atom is 0.1–0.3 nm across; its nucleus is tens of thousands of times smaller. Electrons live in probability clouds and can only have certain energies, like rungs on a ladder. Molecules add smaller rungs for vibration (infrared-sized) and rotation (microwave-sized). In solids the rungs merge into energy bands separated by band gaps.

→ CHAPTERS 4 AND 5

5

One question decides what light does to matter

Ask: is there an allowed jump whose energy matches the photon’s \(hf\)? If yes, the photon is absorbed, and its energy is re-emitted, turned into heat, used for chemistry, or spent knocking out an electron. If no, the wave just shakes the electrons, and they re-radiate in step with it, which produces reflection, transmission, refraction and scattering (Figure P.2). Notice that the atom’s size isn’t in the question: an atom 5,000× smaller than a light wave absorbs it fine, because absorption is about matching energy (resonance).

→ CHAPTERS 5, 6 AND 8

6

Color is what survives

A material absorbs the wavelengths that match its jumps; your eye gets what’s left (a leaf absorbs red and blue, returns green). For solids: band gap above 3.1 eV, no visible absorption (pure diamond is clear); below 1.8 eV, all of it absorbed (silicon looks gray and opaque); in between, colored. The blue sky is different: air molecules scatter short wavelengths far more than long ones.

→ CHAPTER 7

7

Waves spread out and interfere

Light bends around obstacles and spreads through openings (diffraction), and overlapping waves reinforce or cancel (interference). So you can’t image details smaller than about half a wavelength, which is why no light microscope shows atoms. Objects much smaller than the wavelength barely scatter it, which is why a microwave oven’s door mesh blocks 12 cm microwaves but passes visible light.

→ CHAPTER 8

8

Everything glows, and warm things glow in infrared

Everything above absolute zero emits thermal radiation set by its temperature: you (310 K) peak near 9–10 µm in the infrared, a stove burner (~1000 K) glows dull red, the Sun (5800 K) peaks in the visible. IR is called “heat radiation” because warm everyday objects emit mostly IR and molecules absorb it straight into vibration. But IR is light, not heat, and visible light heats things too.

→ CHAPTER 9

9

Above about 10 eV, light becomes ionizing

A photon with more energy than an electron’s binding energy (roughly 10 eV, far UV) can knock it off its atom, breaking bonds and damaging DNA. At everyday intensities, more brightness can’t substitute for photon energy. “Radiation” also means fast particles (alpha, beta, neutrons, cosmic rays). Magnetic fields steer charged particles away, as Earth’s magnetosphere does, but do nothing to photons; only matter stops those.

→ CHAPTER 10

10

X-rays fit between the atoms

X-ray wavelengths (~0.1 nm) are about the size of atoms. That gives them two famous tricks: they pass through soft tissue but not bone, making shadow images, and they diffract off the regular rows of atoms in a solid, reflecting only at angles that satisfy Bragg’s law, \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\). That is how we measure how atoms are arranged.

→ CHAPTER 11

Key Idea — Three equations carry most of this book

\( c = \lambda f \) (wavelength and frequency are locked together) · \( E = hf = \dfrac{hc}{\lambda} \) (photon energy grows with frequency) · \( E_\text{photon} = \Delta E_\text{jump} \) (light is absorbed or emitted only when its photon energy matches an allowed jump). Nearly everything else is these three applied to a particular wavelength and material.

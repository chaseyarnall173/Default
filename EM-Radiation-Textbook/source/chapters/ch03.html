Chapter 3

The Electromagnetic Spectrum

Radio, microwaves, the warmth of a campfire, the color red, sunburn and a dental X-ray feel like completely different things. They are one thing: the wave from Chapter 1, made of the photons from Chapter 2, at wavelengths that span more than eighteen powers of ten.

The big picture

  • Every band is the same phenomenon: EM waves traveling at \(c\), delivered as photons. Only the wavelength differs, and with it the frequency and photon energy.
  • Each band is made by a different kind of accelerating charge, from electrons sloshing in antennas to nuclei rearranging.
  • Photon energy decides what a band does to matter: low energies move charges and make heat, a few eV rearranges electrons (color and chemistry), and above about 10 eV photons ionize.
  • The atmosphere is transparent only in a few windows, mainly visible light and radio.
  • Visible light is a sliver less than one octave wide, and there are good physical reasons our eyes use it.

  • Convert between \(\lambda\), \(f\) and \(E\) for any band.
  • Name typical sources and uses of each band.
  • Predict from photon energy what a band does to matter.
  • Explain which bands reach the ground and why.
  • Explain why human eyes see 400–700 nm.

3.1One phenomenon, many names

You Asked

“What is the EM spectrum — are radio waves, light and X-rays really the same thing?”

Yes. Radio waves, visible light and X-rays are exactly the same phenomenon: oscillating electric and magnetic fields traveling at \(c\), delivered in photons of energy \(hf\). The electromagnetic spectrum is simply the full range of these waves, arranged by wavelength.

What differs is only the wavelength, and therefore the frequency and the energy per photon. Those numbers are not minor details, though. They decide how a wave is made, what can detect it, and what it does when it hits matter. A radio wave is meters long and gently drives electrons in an antenna. An X-ray is smaller than an atom and carries enough energy per photon to knock electrons out of it. Same physics, very different behavior, the way a bass note and a dog whistle are both sound.

Figure 3.1 lays out the whole spectrum. Each band can be labeled three equivalent ways, linked by the two equations you already know:

\[ c = \lambda f \qquad\qquad E = hf = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{\lambda} \]

Radio engineers usually quote frequency, optics people quote wavelength, and X-ray and nuclear physicists quote photon energy. They are all describing the same axis.

Analogy

Think of a piano keyboard stretched for 60 octaves instead of 7. Every key plays the same kind of thing, a vibration, just faster or slower. Visible light is less than one octave of that keyboard: 700 nm to 400 nm is a frequency ratio of only 1.75.

Worked Example 3.1 — How big is the visible slice?

Given: visible light spans 400–700 nm. Figure 3.1 spans wavelengths from \(10^{4}\) m to \(10^{-14}\) m.

Find: what fraction of Figure 3.1’s width is visible, and how many octaves (factors of 2) the figure covers.

Think: On a log scale, width is measured in powers of ten (decades), so use logarithms.

\[ \log_{10}\!\left(\frac{700}{400}\right) = 0.24\ \text{decade}, \qquad \frac{0.24}{18\ \text{decades}} \approx 1.3\% \]

\[ \text{octaves} = \log_2\!\left(10^{18}\right) = 18 \times 3.32 \approx 60 \]

Answer: visible light is about 1% of the figure’s width, under one octave out of roughly 60.

3.2Where each band comes from

Chapter 1’s rule still holds everywhere: accelerating charges make EM waves. What changes across the spectrum is which charge is accelerating, and how fast it oscillates (Figure 3.2). Bigger, slower systems make long waves; smaller, tighter systems make short, high-energy ones.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: X-rays and gamma rays are different kinds of radiation, and gamma rays always have more energy.

Reality: Both are high-energy photons, and their energy ranges overlap. The names usually describe the source: X-rays come from electrons (braking or jumping between inner shells), gamma rays from atomic nuclei. A 150 keV medical X-ray photon has more energy than some gamma rays from nuclear decay.

3.3A tour of the spectrum

Table 3.1 is the one-page summary of the spectrum, from longest to shortest wavelength. A few highlights:

  • Radio and microwaves carry nearly all wireless communication. Wi-Fi (2.4 and 5 GHz), Bluetooth, phones and GPS are microwaves; a microwave oven runs at 2.45 GHz, in the same band as Wi-Fi.
  • Infrared is emitted by everything warm. Your body glows most strongly near 10 µm (Chapter 9), and modeling the Sun as a 5800 K glowing body, about half of its power is infrared.
  • Visible light is where the Sun is brightest and where molecules have their electronic transitions, which is why it carries color (Chapter 7).
  • Ultraviolet photons carry enough energy to break chemical bonds, which causes sunburn and is why UV-C lamps sterilize surfaces.
  • X-rays and gamma rays ionize atoms and penetrate deeply (Chapters 10 and 11).

Worked Example 3.2 — Name that band

Given: (a) a microwave oven at 2.45 GHz; (b) the infrared glow of your skin at about 10 µm; (c) a dental X-ray photon of 60 keV.

Find: the missing wavelength or frequency, and the photon energy, for each; check the band in Table 3.1.

Think: Use \(c = \lambda f\), then \(E = hf\) (with \(h = 4.136\times10^{-15}\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{s}\)) or \(E = 1240/\lambda\).

(a) \(\lambda = \dfrac{3.00\times10^8}{2.45\times10^9} = 12.2\ \text{cm}\), \(\;E = (4.136\times10^{-15})(2.45\times10^{9}) = 1.0\times10^{-5}\ \text{eV}\): microwave.

(b) \(f = \dfrac{3.00\times10^8}{10\times10^{-6}} = 3.0\times10^{13}\ \text{Hz}\), \(\;E = \dfrac{1240}{10{,}000\ \text{nm}} = 0.124\ \text{eV}\): infrared.

(c) \(\lambda = \dfrac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{60{,}000\ \text{eV}} = 0.021\ \text{nm}\), \(\;f = \dfrac{c}{\lambda} = 1.5\times10^{19}\ \text{Hz}\): X-ray.

Answer: 12.2 cm and \(10^{-5}\) eV (microwave); \(3.0\times10^{13}\) Hz and 0.12 eV (infrared); 0.021 nm and \(1.5\times10^{19}\) Hz (X-ray, close to the gamma boundary, but called an X-ray because electrons in a tube make it).

3.4Photon energy decides what light does to matter

Matter absorbs a photon efficiently when the photon’s energy matches a step that something in the material can take. That step might be a molecule spinning faster, a bond vibrating harder, an electron jumping to a higher level or an electron leaving altogether. If nothing matches, the light passes through, reflects or scatters instead (Chapter 6). Figure 3.3 lines up those steps against the spectrum.

Key Idea

Photon energy, not brightness, decides what kind of interaction is possible. Below about 1 eV, light mostly makes things jiggle, which is heat. From about 1.8 to 10 eV it can rearrange outer electrons, which gives color and drives chemistry. Above about 10 eV it can rip electrons off atoms. Because absorption happens one photon at a time (Chapter 2), no number of low-energy photons adds up to one high-energy photon.

Worked Example 3.3 — Which light can break a carbon–carbon bond?

Given: a C–C single bond takes about 3.6 eV to break. Candidates: a germicidal UV-C lamp (254 nm), a green laser (532 nm) and Wi-Fi (2.4 GHz, \(9.9\times10^{-6}\) eV per photon from Worked Example 2.1).

Find: which photons can break the bond in one absorption.

Think: One photon, one event. Compare each photon’s energy with 3.6 eV.

\[ E_\text{UV-C} = \frac{1240}{254} = 4.88\ \text{eV} \gt 3.6\ \text{eV}, \qquad E_\text{green} = \frac{1240}{532} = 2.33\ \text{eV} \lt 3.6\ \text{eV} \]

A Wi-Fi photon falls short by a factor of \(3.6/(9.9\times10^{-6}) \approx 360{,}000\). It is even about 2600 times smaller than the random thermal energy every molecule already has at room temperature, \(k_BT \approx 0.026\) eV.

Answer: only the UV-C photon can break the bond, which is why UV-C damages DNA and kills microbes. Green light and Wi-Fi cannot, at any brightness; their absorbed energy just becomes heat.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Wi-Fi and microwaves are “radiation,” so they are dangerous in the same way as X-rays.

Reality: All light is electromagnetic radiation, including the light you read by. What matters is photon energy and intensity. Radio, microwave, infrared and visible photons are non-ionizing: they cannot break molecules apart one photon at a time, and their only effect at high intensity is heating. Ionizing radiation, photons above about 10 eV, is a separate category (Chapter 10).

Wavelength also matters through size. Waves bend around objects smaller than or similar to their wavelength and are blocked by openings much smaller than it. That is why AM radio, hundreds of meters long, wraps around buildings, and why the mesh in a microwave oven door blocks 12 cm microwaves but passes 500 nm light (Chapter 8).

Materials Science Connection

Look at Figure 3.1 again: X-ray wavelengths are about the size of an atom. Copper Kα X-rays have \(\lambda = 0.1542\) nm, while the spacing between planes of atoms in metals is a few tenths of a nanometer (BCC iron’s lattice parameter is 0.2866 nm). That size match is what makes X-ray diffraction work: crystal planes scatter X-rays like a grating, and the angles reveal the crystal structure and Miller indices (Chapter 11). Visible light, thousands of times longer, simply cannot resolve atomic planes.

3.5What reaches the ground

Earth’s atmosphere absorbs most of the spectrum. Only a few atmospheric windows let radiation reach the surface (Figure 3.4):

  • Gamma rays, X-rays and short UV are absorbed high in the atmosphere. Ozone and oxygen remove UV shorter than about 290 nm (all UV-C and most UV-B); longer UV-A mostly gets through.
  • Visible and near-infrared light pass through, apart from scattering (Chapter 7) and some water-vapor absorption bands.
  • Most infrared is absorbed by water vapor and CO₂, except windows near 3–5 µm and 8–14 µm. That absorption is the greenhouse effect at work (Chapter 9).
  • Radio waves from about 1 cm to roughly 30 m pass freely, even through clouds. Longer waves (below about 10 MHz) are reflected by the ionosphere, which is how AM broadcasts can skip far over the horizon at night.

This is why astronomy splits in two. Optical and radio telescopes work from the ground. X-ray, gamma-ray and most ultraviolet and far-infrared observatories must fly above the atmosphere, which is one reason the James Webb Space Telescope observes infrared from space.

3.6Why your eyes see 400–700 nm

Out of 60 octaves, why did eyes settle on less than one? No single reason explains it, but several physical facts line up (Figure 3.5):

  • The light is there. Sunlight is intense in this band, and the atmosphere is transparent to it.
  • Water is clear there. Water absorbs strongly in the UV and infrared but very little between about 400 and 700 nm. Eyes are mostly water, and the first eyes evolved in water.
  • The photon energy is just right. Vision starts when a single photon flips the shape of a retinal molecule in your retina, a switch that takes about 200 femtoseconds. Photons of 1.8–3.1 eV can do this reliably without breaking other bonds.
  • Too much energy is harmful. UV photons damage molecules, so your cornea and lens absorb almost all UV before it reaches the retina.
  • Too little energy is unreliable. An infrared-tuned pigment would need such a small trigger that the random thermal jostling of a 37°C eye would set it off, and your own body glows in the infrared.

Other animals stretch the window a little. Many insects, including bees, see into the near ultraviolet, which reveals patterns on flowers that are invisible to you.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — Where does sunlight really peak?

Safe to skip. You will often read that “the eye peaks where the Sun peaks.” It is half true. Plotted per nanometer of wavelength, as in Figure 3.5, sunlight peaks near 500 nm. Plotted per hertz of frequency, the same sunlight peaks in the near infrared, around 880 nm. Neither plot is wrong: they divide the spectrum into different-sized bins. The robust statement is the one in the bullets above: the visible band sits where sunlight is plentiful, water and air are clear, and photon energies suit molecular switches.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Visible light is a special kind of radiation, different from the invisible kinds.

Reality: Visible light differs from infrared or ultraviolet only in wavelength. It is “special” only because it is the band your retina’s molecules respond to. A thermal camera, a radio and a dental X-ray sensor are simply detectors for other parts of the same spectrum.

Chapter summary

  • The EM spectrum is one phenomenon spread over more than 18 powers of ten in wavelength. Each band can be labeled by \(\lambda\), \(f = c/\lambda\) or \(E = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}/\lambda\).
  • Order, long to short: radio (> 30 cm), microwave, infrared (700 nm–1 mm), visible (400–700 nm, 1.8–3.1 eV), ultraviolet (10–400 nm), X-ray (0.01–10 nm), gamma ray. Boundaries are conventions; X-ray and gamma overlap and are named by source.
  • Each band is made by accelerating charge at a different scale: antennas, vibrating molecules, outer electrons, fast or inner-shell electrons, nuclei.
  • Photon energy sets the interaction: heating below ~1 eV, electronic and chemical changes from ~1.8 to 10 eV, ionization above ~10 eV.
  • The atmosphere passes visible/near-IR light and radio from ~1 cm to ~30 m; it blocks gamma, X-rays, most UV and most IR.
  • Eyes use 400–700 nm because sunlight is plentiful there, air and water are clear, and those photon energies can flip retinal molecules without damaging them.

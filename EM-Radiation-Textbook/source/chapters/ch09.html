Chapter 9

Infrared, Heat, and Thermal Radiation

You are glowing right now, about 700 watts’ worth, just not in a color your eyes can see. Thermal radiation explains why a stove glows red, why a thermal camera can’t see through a window, and how a spacecraft gets rid of heat with nothing around it.

The big picture

  • Everything above absolute zero emits light because its jiggling charges radiate. Hotter objects emit far more (\(\propto T^4\)) and at shorter wavelengths (\(\propto 1/T\)).
  • At everyday temperatures that glow is infrared, peaking near 10 µm. Objects only glow visibly above about 800 K.
  • Infrared is light, not heat. It earns the nickname “heat radiation” because warm things emit it and molecules absorb it eagerly.
  • Emissivity sets how well a surface emits and absorbs. That one property explains thermal cameras, thermoses, space blankets, and spacecraft radiators.

  • Distinguish heat, temperature, and thermal radiation.
  • Use Wien’s law and the Stefan–Boltzmann law to find peak wavelengths and radiated power.
  • Explain the red-hot to white-hot color sequence and the color temperature of light bulbs.
  • Explain why infrared is called heat radiation, and why that is only half true.
  • Explain thermal cameras, emissivity, the greenhouse effect, microwave ovens, and radiative cooling in space.

9.1Heat, temperature, and radiation

These three words get blurred together in everyday speech, but they mean different things:

  • Thermal energy is the energy of the random jiggling of atoms and molecules.
  • Temperature measures how vigorous that jiggling is on average. At 300 K, a typical molecule’s share of thermal energy is about \(k_BT \approx 0.026\) eV.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. It moves by conduction (contact), convection (moving fluid), or radiation (light).

Thermal radiation is the only one of the three that works through empty space. It is how the Sun warms Earth, and the only way a spacecraft can shed heat.

9.2Everything glows

Every atom contains charges, and in a warm object those charges are constantly being jostled and accelerated. Accelerating charges radiate (Chapter 1). Because thermal jiggling is random, it contains a whole spread of frequencies, so the emitted light is a smooth, continuous spectrum rather than the sharp lines of Chapter 5.

Analogy

Thermal radiation is like the roar of a stadium crowd: no single note, just random voices at every pitch. A more excited crowd is louder and its roar shifts higher, and a hotter object likewise glows brighter and bluer.

An ideal blackbody absorbs every wavelength that hits it. It turns out that a perfect absorber is also the best possible emitter, and its spectrum depends on nothing but its temperature. Max Planck’s formula for that spectrum (1900), shown in Figure 9.1, was the first step toward quantum physics.

Two rules capture Figure 9.1. Wien’s displacement law gives where the peak sits:

\[ \lambda_\text{peak} = \frac{2898\,\mu\text{m}\cdot\text{K}}{T} \]

The Stefan–Boltzmann law gives the total power radiated:

\[ P = \varepsilon\,\sigma A T^{4} \]

where \(T\) is in kelvins, \(A\) is the surface area, \(\sigma = 5.67\times10^{-8}\) W/(m²K⁴), and \(\varepsilon\) is the emissivity (1 for a blackbody, less for real surfaces; Section 9.5).

Since your surroundings radiate back at you, the net loss for an object at \(T\) in surroundings at \(T_\text{s}\) is

\[ P_\text{net} = \varepsilon\,\sigma A\left(T^{4} - T_\text{s}^{4}\right) \]
Worked Example 9.1 — Where you, a stove, and the Sun peak

Given: human body \(T = 310\) K; red-hot stove element \(T \approx 1000\) K; Sun’s surface \(T \approx 5800\) K.

Find: the peak wavelength of each.

Think: Wien’s law (Eq. 9.1), with temperatures in kelvins.

\[ \lambda_\text{you} = \frac{2898}{310}\,\mu\text{m} = 9.35\,\mu\text{m}, \qquad \lambda_\text{stove} = \frac{2898}{1000}\,\mu\text{m} = 2.9\,\mu\text{m}, \qquad \lambda_\text{Sun} = \frac{2898}{5800}\,\mu\text{m} = 0.500\,\mu\text{m} \]

Answer: about 9.3 µm (deep infrared), 2.9 µm (infrared), and 500 nm (blue-green, the middle of the visible band). Even a glowing stove puts out most of its energy as invisible infrared; the red you see is just the tail of its spectrum.

Worked Example 9.2 — How much you radiate

Given: skin at 33.0 °C (306 K), area \(A = 1.50\) m², emissivity of skin \(\varepsilon = 0.97\); room walls at 22.0 °C (295 K). (These are OpenStax’s example values.)

Find: the power you emit, the power you absorb from the room, and the net loss.

Think: Eq. 9.2 for each direction; Eq. 9.3 for the difference.

\[ P_\text{out} = (0.97)(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.50)(306)^4 \approx 723\ \text{W}, \qquad P_\text{in} = (0.97)(5.67\times10^{-8})(1.50)(295)^4 \approx 625\ \text{W} \]

\[ P_\text{net} = 723\ \text{W} - 625\ \text{W} \approx 99\ \text{W} \]

Answer: you emit about 720 W of infrared and absorb about 620 W back from the room, a net loss of about 100 W. That is comparable to your resting metabolic power (roughly 100 W), which is why radiation is one of the main ways your body sheds heat.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — The blackbody puzzle that started quantum physics

Safe to skip. Classical physics predicted that a hot object should emit ever more power at ever shorter wavelengths, without limit, which would make every warm object blaze with ultraviolet. (This was later nicknamed the “ultraviolet catastrophe.”) Planck fixed it in 1900 by assuming energy is emitted in chunks of \(hf\). A high-frequency chunk costs far more than the typical thermal energy \(k_BT\), so those modes are rarely excited, and the spectrum turns over and dies away. That is why your 310 K body emits infrared and essentially no visible light or X-rays. The chunks became photons (Chapter 2).

Key Idea

Every object emits thermal radiation all the time. Double the absolute temperature and it emits 16 times the power, with its peak at half the wavelength. Whether something warms or cools depends on the net exchange with its surroundings.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Cold objects don’t give off radiation, so a cold window must be “radiating cold” at you.

Reality: Ice at 0 °C still emits about 315 W per square meter. There is no such thing as “cold rays.” You feel chilly near a cold window because you radiate more energy to it than it radiates back to you.

9.3Red-hot, white-hot, and the color of light bulbs

As something heats up, the short-wavelength tail of its spectrum climbs into the visible range red end first (Figure 9.1b). Around 800 K (about 525 °C, the Draper point) the glow first becomes visible in a dark room as a dull red. Hotter still, more of the visible range fills in: the glow turns orange, then yellow, then white once the whole range is well covered (Figure 9.2). The brightness also rockets upward, by a factor of more than a million between 800 K and 1500 K in the visible range.

Cherry red≈ 800–900≈ 1100–1150

White≈ 1300 and up≈ 1550 and up

Light bulbs are labeled by color temperature: the temperature of a blackbody with the same color. An incandescent filament really is that hot, about 2700–3300 K. Even so, its Wien peak is near 1 µm, in the infrared, and only about 5–8% of its output falls in the visible band. That is why incandescent bulbs waste most of their energy as heat. An LED marked “2700 K warm white” only mimics that color; the LED itself stays near room temperature. Daylight is around 5000–6500 K. Note the naming is backwards from the physics: “warm” light matches a cooler glow than “cool” light.

9.4Why infrared gets called “heat radiation”

In 1800 William Herschel spread sunlight into a spectrum and moved thermometers through the colors. The biggest temperature rise came just beyond the red, where he could see nothing. These “heat rays” were infrared light, and the name stuck for two real reasons.

  1. Warm things emit infrared. Anything between about 250 K and 500 K, including you, your house, the ground, and a hot pan, emits almost all its thermal radiation in the infrared (Wien peaks of about 6–12 µm). The radiation you feel from a warm object is infrared.
  2. Molecules absorb infrared and turn it straight into heat. Infrared photons (about 0.01–1 eV) match the energy steps of molecular vibrations (Chapter 5). Water, skin, glass, and CO2 absorb them strongly, often within micrometers to millimeters, and the vibration is quickly shared by collisions as random motion (Figure 9.3).

But “heat radiation” is only half true. Any light that is absorbed ends up as thermal energy. A black car in the sun gets hot mostly from visible and near-infrared sunlight (Chapter 7), and microwaves heat your food (Section 9.7). Sunlight reaching the ground is roughly half infrared and half visible, with a few percent UV.

You Asked

“Is infrared the same thing as heat?”

No. Infrared is light, an electromagnetic wave just like visible light but with longer wavelengths (about 0.7 µm to 1 mm). Heat is energy being transferred because of a temperature difference, and thermal energy is the random jiggling of atoms.

They get linked for two reasons: objects at everyday temperatures emit mostly infrared (Wien’s law), and infrared photons match molecular vibration energies, so materials like water, skin, glass, and CO2 absorb them and turn them directly into random thermal motion. The link isn’t exclusive. Visible light heats a black car, and microwaves heat food. Infrared can also carry information with no noticeable heating, like the near-infrared beam from your TV remote.

You Asked

“Why are infrared waves often called ‘heat waves’?”

Because infrared is the radiation warm things give off and the radiation your skin feels as warmth. The name dates to Herschel’s 1800 thermometer experiment. Physically, it is light that happens to match the energies of molecular vibrations, so it is absorbed strongly and becomes heat almost immediately (see the previous box and Figure 9.3). “Heat radiation” describes what infrared usually does, not what it is.

9.5Thermal cameras and emissivity

You Asked

“Why does infrared show heat?”

Because at everyday temperatures the light an object emits by itself is almost entirely infrared, and the amount rises steeply with temperature (\(\propto T^4\)). Measure an object’s infrared glow and you have measured its temperature, from a distance. A normal camera records reflected visible light, which tells you about color, not temperature. A thermal camera records each object’s own emission, so its image is essentially a temperature map.

Most thermal cameras detect the 8–14 µm band. That band sits right on the emission peak of room-temperature objects, and it coincides with a window where air is nearly transparent. Each pixel is a tiny microbolometer, a speck of material that warms slightly when infrared lands on it and changes its electrical resistance. The lenses are made of germanium, not glass, for a reason that shows up in Figure 9.4b.

Why glass blocks thermal cameras. Glass is transparent to visible light because visible photons can’t excite its electrons (Chapter 6). But its silicon–oxygen bonds vibrate at frequencies matching light near 9 µm (Chapter 6), so it absorbs strongly in exactly the thermal camera’s band. By Kirchhoff’s law (Section 9.3), a strong absorber is also a strong emitter. The camera sees the glass glowing at its own temperature, with reflections on top.

Why you can’t see through walls. Walls are opaque to infrared too, so a thermal camera only ever sees a wall’s surface temperature. It can reveal a hot pipe, a stud, or missing insulation, but only because those change how heat conducts to the surface. It cannot see people in the next room.

Emissivity (\(\varepsilon\), from 0 to 1) measures how well a surface emits compared with a blackbody. For an opaque surface, whatever it doesn’t absorb it reflects, so a low-emissivity surface is a good mirror for infrared (Table 9.2). That is why a shiny pan “looks cold” to a thermal camera (Figure 9.4a). Technicians fix this with a strip of matte tape or paint.

Key Idea

Good absorbers are good emitters, and good reflectors are poor emitters (Kirchhoff’s law). A thermal camera reads a surface’s own glow only when its emissivity is high; on shiny metal it mostly sees reflections.

9.6The greenhouse effect

Earth absorbs sunlight, mostly visible and near-infrared, and must radiate the same power back to space as thermal infrared near 10 µm. The two bands barely overlap (Figure 9.5), so the atmosphere can treat them very differently. Nitrogen and oxygen, 99% of dry air, are symmetric two-atom molecules: stretching them shifts no charge, so they are essentially transparent to infrared (Chapter 5). CO2 and H2O have vibrations that absorb thermal infrared strongly, CO2 especially around 15 µm, close to the peak of Earth’s glow.

The absorbing molecules re-emit in all directions, so part of Earth’s outgoing infrared is sent back down. The surface has to warm up until enough radiation leaks out to balance the incoming sunlight. In the simplest energy-balance model, Earth without greenhouse gases would average about 255 K (−18 °C); the actual average is about 288 K (15 °C). Adding CO2 widens and deepens the absorbed bands, so the balance point moves warmer.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: The atmosphere warms Earth the same way a glass greenhouse warms plants.

Reality: A real greenhouse stays warm mainly because its walls stop warm air from rising and blowing away; it blocks convection. The atmospheric greenhouse effect is radiative: gases absorb and re-emit outgoing infrared. Same name, different mechanism.

9.7Microwave ovens

A microwave oven fills its cavity with 2.45 GHz waves (\(\lambda = 12.2\) cm). Each photon carries only about \(10^{-5}\) eV, far too little to break a chemical bond or ionize anything (Chapter 10). The heating works through the oscillating electric field instead. Water molecules are electric dipoles, and the field tries to twist them back and forth 2.45 billion times per second.

In liquid water, each molecule is caught in a jostling network of hydrogen bonds, so it can’t rotate freely and lags behind the field. Because of that lag, the field keeps doing work on the molecules, and the energy is lost as random motion, which is heat. This is dielectric heating. Its strength varies smoothly with frequency, with a broad maximum near 20 GHz at room temperature (Figure 9.6). Ice heats poorly because its molecules are locked in a crystal and can barely turn, which is why defrost settings cycle on and off to let heat conduct inward.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Microwave ovens are tuned to the resonant frequency of water molecules.

Reality: Liquid water has no sharp resonance at 2.45 GHz. Its absorption is a broad relaxation hump that peaks near 20 GHz. Ovens use 2.45 GHz because that band is allocated for industrial and home heating, and because water absorbs it only moderately, so the waves penetrate a centimeter or more into food. At 20 GHz the outer skin would absorb nearly everything and the inside would stay cold.

9.8Controlling thermal radiation: thermoses, space blankets, and radiators

Keeping heat in. A thermos (vacuum flask) blocks all three heat paths. The vacuum gap stops conduction and convection, and the silvered, low-emissivity walls barely emit infrared and reflect back what reaches them. A space blanket is plastic film coated with a thin layer of aluminum, with \(\varepsilon\) of only a few percent. It reflects your infrared back toward you and emits very little outward. Spacecraft wrap themselves in multilayer insulation, many such aluminized layers separated by vacuum.

Getting heat out. In space there is no air, so a spacecraft can only lose heat by radiating it. The deep-space background is only about 3 K. Spacecraft pipe waste heat (on the International Space Station, through liquid ammonia loops) to radiator panels coated with high-emissivity white paint. White paint seems like a strange choice for a radiator, but it reflects most visible sunlight while emitting strongly in the infrared. That is possible because emissivity can be very different at 0.5 µm and at 10 µm.

Worked Example 9.3 — Sizing a spacecraft radiator

Given: electronics produce 1.0 kW of waste heat; a radiator at 300 K with \(\varepsilon = 0.90\) faces deep space (background ≈ 3 K) on one side; ignore sunlight.

Find: the radiator area needed.

Think: in steady state, radiated power equals waste heat. The 3 K background radiates back almost nothing (\(3^4\) is negligible next to \(300^4\)), so Eq. 9.2 alone is enough.

\[ \frac{P}{A} = \varepsilon\sigma T^4 = (0.90)(5.67\times10^{-8})(300)^4 = 413\ \text{W/m}^2 \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad A = \frac{1000\ \text{W}}{413\ \text{W/m}^2} = 2.4\ \text{m}^2 \]

Answer: about 2.4 m², the size of a large tabletop for just 1 kW. That is why spacecraft with big power systems carry huge radiator wings.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Space is so cold that an unprotected person would freeze solid instantly.

Reality: Vacuum is an excellent insulator. With no air for conduction or convection, a body loses heat only by radiation, a few hundred watts, so cooling takes a long time. In direct sunlight, overheating is the bigger problem. Spacecraft thermal design is mostly about dumping heat, not keeping warm.

Chapter summary

  • Thermal energy is random atomic motion, temperature measures its average, and heat is energy transferred by a temperature difference. Only radiation works through vacuum.
  • Jiggling charges make every object emit a continuous thermal spectrum. Wien’s law gives \(\lambda_\text{peak} = 2898\ \mu\text{m·K}/T\); Stefan–Boltzmann gives \(P = \varepsilon\sigma AT^4\). You peak near 9–10 µm and radiate about 100 W net.
  • Visible glow starts near 800 K (dull red), then goes orange, yellow, and white. Bulbs are labeled by color temperature, and “warm” light means a lower temperature.
  • Infrared is light, not heat. It is called heat radiation because everyday objects emit it and molecular vibrations absorb it, but any absorbed light, visible or microwave, becomes heat.
  • Thermal cameras read 8–14 µm emission. Glass and walls are opaque there, and low-emissivity shiny surfaces mostly show reflections. Kirchhoff’s law: good absorbers are good emitters.
  • Greenhouse gases (CO2, H2O) pass sunlight but absorb Earth’s outgoing infrared: about 255 K without them, about 288 K with them.
  • Microwave ovens heat by dielectric relaxation of water (a broad hump near 20 GHz), not a sharp resonance. Low-ε surfaces keep heat in; high-ε radiators dump heat in space.

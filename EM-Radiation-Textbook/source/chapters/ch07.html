Chapter 7

Color

A ripe tomato, a gold ring, the blue sky, and a soap bubble are colored for four completely different physical reasons. Once you can name the mechanism, you can predict the color.

The big picture

  • Color is your visual system's summary of which visible wavelengths (400–700 nm, photon energies 1.8–3.1 eV) reach your eye.
  • Three types of cone cells turn any spectrum into just three signals, so very different spectra can look identical.
  • Most objects are colored by subtraction: they absorb some wavelengths and send back the rest.
  • Absorption happens where an electron energy gap matches a visible photon: band gaps, dye molecules, impurity ions, and metals' bands.
  • Scattering, interference, and emission make color with no pigment at all.

After this chapter you can

  • Explain color in terms of wavelengths, photon energies, and cone responses.
  • Distinguish additive (light) from subtractive (pigment) color mixing.
  • Predict a material's appearance from its band gap using the 1.8 eV / 3.1 eV rule.
  • Explain the colors of ruby, sapphire, gold, copper, and silver.
  • Compute relative Rayleigh scattering and explain blue skies, red sunsets, and white clouds.

7.1Color is light plus a brain

Visible light spans wavelengths of about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). Using the shortcut from Chapter 2, those are photon energies from 3.1 eV down to 1.8 eV:

\[ E = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{\lambda} \]

Light from almost any object is a mixture of many wavelengths, its spectrum. "Color" is the label your eye and brain attach to that mixture.

You Asked

“How does color work in terms of EM radiation?”

Color is how your visual system labels the mix of visible wavelengths (photon energies of about 1.8 to 3.1 eV) entering your eye; an object's color is whatever part of the light falling on it the object sends toward you.

Three steps set what you see:

  • The source provides a spectrum: sunlight contains every visible wavelength; a sodium streetlight is almost pure 589 nm yellow.
  • The object reflects, absorbs, transmits, or scatters each wavelength differently (Chapter 6). What survives travels to you.
  • Your eye measures the arriving light with three kinds of cones, and your brain compares the three signals (Section 7.2).

Change any step and the color changes. That is why a red car looks muddy brown under a sodium lamp: no red light is there for it to send back.

7.2Three cones, three numbers

Your retina has three types of cone cells, each with a pigment tuned to a different range (Figure 7.1): S (short-wavelength) cones peak near 420 nm, M near 534 nm, and L near 564 nm. Rods, which peak near 498 nm, handle dim light and do not distinguish color.

Your brain never sees the full spectrum, only three numbers: how strongly S, M, and L respond. Any two spectra that produce the same three numbers look identical. Such spectra are called metamers, and they are why a screen can show yellow using only red and green light.

Analogy

Describing a spectrum with three cone signals is like describing a song only by the loudness of its bass, midrange, and treble. Very different songs can give the same three numbers.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Every color you can see corresponds to some wavelength of light.

Reality: Magenta, pink, brown, and white appear nowhere in the rainbow. Magenta is what your brain reports when L and S cones fire but M cones do not, a combination no single wavelength can produce.

7.3Mixing color: adding light vs. subtracting it

There are two opposite ways to mix color (Figure 7.2):

  • Additive mixing combines lights. Start dark and add red, green, and blue (RGB) light in different amounts. Phone and TV screens work this way.
  • Subtractive mixing combines absorbers: inks, paints, filters. Start with white light; each layer removes part of the spectrum. Cyan ink absorbs red, magenta absorbs green, and yellow absorbs blue (CMY).
Key Idea

Pigments don't add color; they take it away. Yellow plus cyan paint looks green because yellow removes blue and cyan removes red, so only green survives both.

7.4Objects are colored by what they remove

Under white light, an object absorbs some wavelengths and reflects or transmits the rest. The leftovers set its color (Figure 7.3). A red apple absorbs violet through yellow. A leaf's chlorophyll absorbs strongly in the blue (near 430 nm) and red (near 662 nm), leaving mostly green to come back.

Because color is leftover light, illumination matters. Under pure blue light, a red apple has nothing to reflect and looks nearly black.

Key Idea

An object doesn't "have" a color the way it has a mass. It has an absorption spectrum. The color you see is the illumination minus what the object absorbs, as read by your three cones.

Dyes and pigments absorb because their molecules have electron energy gaps in the 1.8–3.1 eV range. The usual culprit is a conjugated chain of alternating single and double bonds, along which some electrons are spread out. Like a longer guitar string, a longer chain gives lower energy levels and smaller gaps, so it absorbs longer wavelengths. Beta-carotene (carrots) has 11 conjugated double bonds and absorbs blue light near 450 nm, so carrots look orange. Molecules with short conjugated chains absorb only UV, which is why most plastics and sugars are colorless.

Absorbed energy mostly turns into heat within picoseconds as the molecule shakes its neighbors. Occasionally it is re-emitted as fluorescence (Chapter 5).

7.5Band gaps and the 1.8–3.1 eV rule

You Asked

“What are the electron energy gaps that give things their color?”

They are the energy differences between states an electron can occupy, and a material is colored when one of its gaps falls between about 1.8 and 3.1 eV, the energies of visible photons. Photons that can bridge the gap are absorbed; the rest come back to you.

Four kinds of gaps do most of the coloring in the world:

  • Band gaps in solids (semiconductors, minerals, pigments): the gap between the filled valence band and the empty conduction band (Chapter 5). Rule below.
  • Gaps between molecular orbitals in dyes, chlorophyll, and carotene; longer conjugated chains give smaller gaps (Section 7.4).
  • Gaps between d-orbital levels of transition-metal ions in a crystal, such as Cr³⁺ in Al₂O₃ (ruby), split by the surrounding ions (Section 7.6).
  • Metals have no gap for their free electrons, so they reflect strongly; their color comes from where deeper electrons begin absorbing, near 2.4 eV in gold (Section 7.7).

For a pure solid with band gap \(E_g\), photons with \(E \gt E_g\) are absorbed and lower-energy photons are not. The absorption edge is at

\[ \lambda_\text{edge} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{E_g} \]

Wavelengths shorter than \(\lambda_\text{edge}\) are absorbed; longer ones pass or reflect.

Comparing \(E_g\) with the visible window gives Callister's rule (Figure 7.4):

  • \(E_g \gt 3.1\) eV: no visible light is absorbed. Pure crystals are colorless and transparent (diamond, 5.5 eV; sapphire, about 9 eV; ZnS, 3.6 eV). Their powders look white.
  • \(E_g \lt 1.8\) eV: every visible photon is absorbed, so the material is opaque. Silicon (1.1 eV) and GaAs (1.4 eV) look gray and metallic, because with \(n \approx 4\) their surfaces still reflect about 35% of the light (Equation 6.4).
  • \(1.8 \lt E_g \lt 3.1\) eV: only the high-energy (blue) end is absorbed. The material looks yellow, orange, or red, shifting toward red as \(E_g\) shrinks.
Worked Example 7.1 — The color of cadmium sulfide

Given: CdS has a band gap \(E_g = 2.42\) eV.

Find: its absorption edge and its color in white light.

Think: photons above \(E_g\) (wavelengths below the edge) are absorbed; the leftovers set the color.

\[ \lambda_\text{edge} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{2.42\ \text{eV}} = 512\ \text{nm} \]

CdS absorbs violet, blue, and blue-green (below 512 nm) and returns green, yellow, orange, and red. With blue missing, the mixture looks yellow to yellow-orange.

Answer: edge at 512 nm; CdS looks yellow-orange. It is the classic artist's pigment "cadmium yellow."

Materials Science Connection

Tuning a band gap. Within a family of compounds, heavier and larger atoms give weaker bonds and smaller gaps: ZnS (3.6 eV, white) → ZnSe (2.7 eV, yellow); CdS (2.4 eV, yellow) → CdSe (1.7 eV, near black). Alloying CdS with CdSe sets the gap anywhere in between, which is how cadmium orange and cadmium red pigments are made. LEDs use the same trick: the emitted photon energy is roughly the band gap (Chapter 5). GaN's 3.4 eV gap would emit 365 nm UV; alloying in indium lowers the gap to about 2.7 eV for the blue (about 450 nm) LEDs in white lights.

7.6Impurity ions: ruby, sapphire, and colored glass

Pure Al₂O₃ has a gap near 9 eV, so it is colorless. Replace up to about 1% of the Al³⁺ ions with Cr³⁺ and you have ruby. Chromium's partly filled 3d orbitals are split into levels about 2–3 eV apart by the surrounding O²⁻ ions (the crystal field). Ruby absorbs violet (near 410 nm) and yellow-green (near 550 nm), transmitting red plus a little blue. Chromium in ruby also fluoresces deep red at 694 nm, the line used in the first laser.

Blue sapphire works differently. Neighboring Fe²⁺ and Ti⁴⁺ ions can swap an electron when a photon supplies about 2 eV (charge transfer). That absorbs a broad band of yellow and orange light, leaving blue. Glassmakers use the same physics: cobalt (Co²⁺) gives deep blue glass, and the iron (Fe²⁺) in ordinary window glass gives its edges a green tint.

Materials Science Connection

In corundum (Al₂O₃), O²⁻ ions form a hexagonal close-packed array and Al³⁺ fills two-thirds of the octahedral interstitial sites. Cr³⁺ has the same charge and a similar size to Al³⁺, so it slips onto Al sites as a substitutional impurity. Callister describes ruby as single-crystal sapphire with about 0.5–2% Cr₂O₃ added. A fraction of a percent of the right ion turns a colorless ceramic into a gemstone, the optical version of how small dopant levels transform a semiconductor's conductivity. The same Cr³⁺ in beryl, where the surrounding field is slightly weaker, shifts the absorption bands and gives emerald green.

7.7Metals: why gold is yellow and silver isn't

All metals reflect strongly because their free electrons respond to every visible frequency (Chapter 6). A metal's color comes from where that reflection drops, which happens where more tightly bound electrons in the filled d band can start absorbing (Figure 7.5):

  • Silver: d-band absorption starts near 4 eV (about 320 nm, in the UV), so all visible colors reflect equally. Silver looks colorless and bright.
  • Gold: absorption starts near 2.4 eV (about 520 nm), removing much of the blue and violet. The remaining red, orange, yellow, and green look yellow.
  • Copper: absorption starts lower still (near 2 eV, about 590 nm), taking out more green and leaving copper's reddish orange.
Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Gold is yellow because it reflects only yellow light.

Reality: Gold reflects red, orange, yellow, and most green almost perfectly. It looks yellow because blue and violet are missing, and white light minus blue looks yellow.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — Gold is yellow because of relativity

Safe to skip. Gold sits directly below silver in the periodic table, so why is its d-band edge at 2.4 eV instead of about 4 eV? Gold's nucleus (Z = 79) is so highly charged that its inner electrons move at a sizable fraction of \(c\). Einstein's relativity then lowers the energy of gold's 6s orbitals and raises its 5d orbitals, shrinking the gap between them from the UV into the blue. Without relativity, gold would look like silver.

7.8Scattering: blue sky, red sunsets, white clouds

Air molecules (N₂ and O₂) are about a thousand times smaller than visible wavelengths. Light drives their electrons, which re-radiate (Chapter 6). Because the molecules are randomly placed in a gas, the sideways wavelets do not fully cancel, and some light is scattered in all directions. For scatterers much smaller than the wavelength, this Rayleigh scattering depends steeply on wavelength (Chapter 8 explains why):

\[ I_\text{scattered} \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4} \]
Worked Example 7.2 — How much more is blue scattered than red?

Given: blue light at 450 nm and red light at 700 nm, equally bright, passing through the same air.

Find: the ratio of the scattered intensities.

Think: air molecules are far smaller than both wavelengths, so Equation 7.3 applies and the constant cancels in a ratio.

\[ \frac{I_{450}}{I_{700}} = \left(\frac{700\ \text{nm}}{450\ \text{nm}}\right)^4 = (1.556)^4 = 5.86 \]

Answer: blue is scattered about 6 times more strongly than red (and 400 nm violet about 9.4 times).

When you look at any patch of sky away from the sun, you see sunlight scattered toward you, which is rich in blue. At sunset, sunlight crosses far more air to reach you; most of the blue is scattered out of the beam on the way, and what arrives directly is orange and red (Figure 7.6b). Dust and smoke add extra scattering and can make sunsets more vivid.

Clouds are white because their water droplets (around 10–20 µm across) are much larger than visible wavelengths. Large droplets scatter all colors about equally, and light bounces among many droplets, so clouds return white light. Very thick clouds look gray because little light makes it through to their bottoms.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: The sky is blue because it reflects the ocean.

Reality: The sky is just as blue over deserts. Its blue is sunlight scattered by air molecules, following Equation 7.3.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — Why isn't the sky violet?

Safe to skip. Violet scatters even more than blue, so why isn't the sky violet? Three reasons add up: sunlight contains less violet than blue, some violet is absorbed high in the atmosphere, and your eyes are much less sensitive to violet (Figure 7.1). Your cones' response to the scattered skylight adds up to sky blue.

7.9Structural color: soap bubbles and butterflies

Some colors come from no pigment at all, only from structure. A soap film reflects light from both its front and back surfaces (Figure 7.7a). The back-surface wave travels an extra distance of about \(2nt\), where \(t\) is the film thickness. Wavelengths whose crests line up are reinforced and others cancel, a wave effect called interference (Chapter 8). As \(t\) varies across the film, so does the color (Figure 7.7b). Where the film thins to a few tens of nanometers, the two reflections cancel for every color and the film looks black, a sign it is about to pop.

Oil slicks on wet pavement work the same way. The brilliant blue of Morpho butterflies comes from ridged nanostructures in their wing scales, with spacing comparable to a quarter of a blue wavelength, rather than from blue pigment. Structural colors usually shift as you tilt your head, an effect called iridescence, because the extra path length changes with angle.

7.10Color from emission: neon, fireworks, LEDs, screens

Things that make their own light have the color of their emission spectrum (Chapter 5):

  • Neon signs: pure neon glows red-orange. Other "neon" colors use argon with a little mercury, whose UV light makes phosphor coatings on the tube glow.
  • Fireworks: metal atoms emit their characteristic lines: strontium red, barium green, copper blue, sodium yellow (589 nm).
  • LEDs emit photons with energy close to the band gap. Most white LEDs are blue LEDs coated with a yellow-emitting phosphor, which absorbs some blue and re-emits yellow (the incoherent absorb-and-re-emit process of Chapter 6). Blue plus yellow looks white.
  • Screens build every pixel from red, green, and blue subpixels and rely on additive mixing and your three cones (Figure 7.2a).

7.11Why black things get hot in the sun

A black surface absorbs most of the sunlight hitting it; a white surface reflects most of it. Absorbed light becomes heat (Section 7.4), so a black car seat can burn your legs while a white one stays tolerable. More than half of sunlight's energy is actually near-infrared, so a surface's heating also depends on wavelengths you can't see. "Cool roof" coatings in dark colors use pigments that reflect near-infrared.

Worked Example 7.3 — Black roof vs. white roof on a car

Given: sunlight of about 1000 W/m² hits a 3.0 m² car roof. A black roof absorbs about 95% of it; a white roof reflects about 70%, so it absorbs 30%.

Find: the absorbed power for each roof.

Think: absorbed power = absorbed fraction × intensity × area.

\[ P_\text{black} = 0.95 \times 1000\ \text{W/m}^2 \times 3.0\ \text{m}^2 = 2850\ \text{W} \]

\[ P_\text{white} = 0.30 \times 1000\ \text{W/m}^2 \times 3.0\ \text{m}^2 = 900\ \text{W} \]

Answer: about 2.9 kW vs. 0.9 kW. The 2 kW difference is more than a typical space heater. How hot each roof gets depends on how fast it loses heat, which is Chapter 9's topic.

Chapter summary

  • Visible light is 400–700 nm, photon energies 3.1–1.8 eV. Color is the brain's reading of three cone signals (S, M, L peaks near 420, 534, 564 nm), so different spectra (metamers) can look identical.
  • Light mixes additively (RGB screens); pigments mix subtractively (CMY inks), because each pigment removes part of the spectrum.
  • Most objects are colored by what they absorb. The absorbed light becomes heat; the leftovers reach your eye.
  • Band-gap rule: \(E_g \gt 3.1\) eV is colorless, \(E_g \lt 1.8\) eV is opaque and dark or metallic, and gaps in between are yellow to red. The edge is at \(\lambda = 1240/E_g\) nm.
  • Impurity ions (Cr³⁺ in ruby, Fe²⁺–Ti⁴⁺ in sapphire) and conjugated molecules (dyes, carotene, chlorophyll) create visible-energy gaps. Gold and copper are colored by d-band absorption starting near 2.4 and 2 eV; silver's starts in the UV.
  • Rayleigh scattering (\(\propto 1/\lambda^4\)) makes the sky blue and sunsets red; large cloud droplets scatter all colors and look white.
  • Thin films and nanostructures make structural color by interference; emitters (neon, fireworks, LEDs, screens) show their emission spectra.

Chapter 5

Absorption and Emission: Electrons Jumping Up and Down

The red of a neon sign, the glow of a highlighter under a black light, the heat of sunlight on your arm, the beam of a laser pointer: each is the same tiny event repeated trillions of times a second. An electron jumps between two allowed energies, and a photon carries the difference.

The big picture

  • An atom absorbs a photon only if the photon’s energy matches a gap between two of its levels: \(hf = E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower}\).
  • When an electron drops, the atom creates a new photon carrying exactly the energy difference.
  • Every element has its own ladder of levels, so every element has its own spectral fingerprint.
  • Absorbed energy is re-emitted, turned into heat, or used for chemistry. Reflection is a different process.
  • Molecules add infrared (vibration) and microwave (rotation) ladders; in solids, the band gap decides metal vs. insulator and an LED’s color.

After this chapter you can

  • Decide from an energy-level diagram which photons an atom can absorb.
  • Compute emitted wavelengths, such as hydrogen’s 656 nm red line.
  • Explain continuous, emission-line and absorption-line spectra.
  • Describe the fates of absorbed energy and distinguish re-emission from reflection.
  • Explain fluorescence, phosphorescence and the Stokes shift.
  • Use band gaps to classify solids and predict LED colors; explain how a laser works.

5.1The one rule: the photon must match a gap

An atom’s electron can sit only on certain rungs of an energy ladder (Chapter 4). Light deals with that ladder through one rule:

\[ E_\text{photon} = hf = \frac{hc}{\lambda} = E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower} \]

where \(h\) is Planck’s constant, \(f\) the light’s frequency and \(\lambda\) its wavelength (Chapter 2).

A photon whose energy matches a gap can be absorbed: it vanishes completely and the electron jumps up (Figure 5.1A). A photon with the wrong energy, too little or too much, cannot drive that jump; it passes by or is weakly scattered (Chapter 6). With \(hc = 1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}\):

\[ \lambda\,(\text{nm}) = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{\Delta E\,(\text{eV})} \]

Why so picky? Resonance. The wave’s electric field pushes the electron cloud back and forth at the light’s frequency. The atom has natural frequencies \(f = \Delta E/h\), one per pair of levels (Section 5.2 shows what oscillates). At a match, every push lands in step and drives the atom up; off resonance, the pushes cancel out over many cycles.

Key Idea

Light is absorbed by resonance: the photon’s energy (its frequency) must match an energy gap. Brighter light delivers more photons, but never changes which gaps a photon can bridge.

Analogy

A swing builds up only if you push at its own rhythm; push at the wrong rhythm, however hard, and it barely moves. An atom is a swing with several fixed rhythms, one per energy gap.

Two refinements. Above the ionization energy the electron is free, so any photon above that energy can be absorbed, the excess becoming kinetic energy. And real lines have a small width, which broadens into wide bands in liquids and solids (Sections 5.7–5.8).

You Asked

“How can an atom that’s thousands of times smaller than a light wave absorb it?”

Because absorption depends on matching the wave’s frequency, not its size. The atom doesn’t need to “catch” the whole wave; it needs the wave to shake it at one of its natural rhythms, \(f = \Delta E/h\).

A 500 nm wave is about 5,000 times longer than an atom, so the whole atom sits in an almost uniform electric field that reverses direction about \(6\times10^{14}\) times per second, shaking the entire cloud in unison, like a cork riding an ocean swell far longer than itself. At resonance the energy builds up and the atom absorbs a photon. In fact, an isolated atom at exact resonance intercepts light from an area roughly the wavelength squared, about a million times its own cross-section. Chapter 8 goes deeper into size versus wavelength.

Worked Example 5.1 — Which photons can ground-state hydrogen absorb?

Given: hydrogen in its ground state (\(E_1 = -13.6\) eV). Photons arrive with energies 10.2, 11.0, 12.1 and 15.0 eV.

Find: which are absorbed, and what happens.

Think: gaps from \(n = 1\) (from \(E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2\)): 10.20 eV to \(n = 2\), 12.09 eV to \(n = 3\), 12.75 eV to \(n = 4\), and 13.6 eV or more to free the electron.

  • 10.2 eV: matches \(1 \to 2\). Absorbed.
  • 11.0 eV: between 10.20 and 12.09 eV; no level fits. Passes through.
  • 12.1 eV: matches \(1 \to 3\). Absorbed.
  • 15.0 eV: above 13.6 eV. Absorbed, ionizing the atom; the electron leaves with 1.4 eV of kinetic energy.

Answer: 10.2, 12.1 and 15.0 eV are absorbed; 11.0 eV is not. Every gap from the ground state is at least 10.2 eV (122 nm, ultraviolet), so ground-state hydrogen ignores all visible light. That is why hydrogen gas is colorless.

5.2Emission: how a jump makes a photon

An excited atom doesn’t stay excited. Typically within nanoseconds (1.6 ns for hydrogen’s 2p state), the electron drops on its own and a photon carries away the difference: spontaneous emission (Figure 5.1B), again obeying Equation 5.1. An electron high on the ladder may drop in one step or several (say \(4 \to 2 \to 1\)), one photon per step.

You Asked

“When an electron jumps down an energy level, how does that make a photon, and what is ‘re-emission’?”

The photon is created during the jump itself; it is not stored inside the atom beforehand. While changing levels, the electron is briefly in a mixture of the upper and lower orbitals, and that mixture’s charge cloud sloshes back and forth at exactly \(f = (E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower})/h\) (Figure 5.2). A sloshing charge is a tiny antenna, and oscillating charges radiate electromagnetic waves (Chapter 1). Over a few nanoseconds the mixture shifts entirely into the lower orbital, and the radiated wave carries away exactly \(\Delta E\): one photon.

Re-emission means an atom that has just absorbed a photon gives the energy back as a new photon, usually of the same energy, nanoseconds later. The new photon has no memory of the old one: it leaves in a random direction with a random phase, which is what separates re-emission from reflection (Section 5.5). (This is the “semiclassical” picture. Full quantum electrodynamics describes the photon as a quantum of the electromagnetic field, but it keeps the key result: the photon’s frequency is the sloshing frequency \(\Delta E/h\).)

A single, pure orbital is a steady cloud with nothing oscillating, so it does not radiate; emission happens only during a jump.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — What makes the electron decide to drop?

Safe to skip. Even empty space has an electromagnetic field with unavoidable quantum jitter, and that jitter nudges an excited atom into the sloshing mixture. Some pairs of orbitals have shapes that barely slosh at all: “forbidden” transitions, whose upper state lingers. Hydrogen’s 2s state lasts about 0.12 s, some 75 million times longer than 2p. Such long-lived metastable states make phosphorescence and lasers possible.

5.3Spectral lines: every element’s fingerprint

Each element has its own ladder, so each emits and absorbs its own spectral lines. For hydrogen, Equation 5.1 with \(E_n = -13.6\ \text{eV}/n^2\) gives every line:

\[ \Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}\left(\frac{1}{n_\text{lower}^2} - \frac{1}{n_\text{upper}^2}\right) \]

Drops ending on \(n = 1\) (the Lyman series) are ultraviolet, on \(n = 2\) (Balmer) visible, and on \(n = 3\) (Paschen) infrared (Figure 5.3).

Worked Example 5.2 — Hydrogen’s red line

Given: an electron in hydrogen drops from \(n = 3\) to \(n = 2\).

Find: the photon’s energy, wavelength and color.

Think: the photon carries exactly the gap (Equation 5.3); Equation 5.2 converts it to wavelength.

\[ \Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}\left(\frac{1}{2^2} - \frac{1}{3^2}\right) = 13.6\ \text{eV}\left(\frac{5}{36}\right) = 1.89\ \text{eV} \]

\[ \lambda = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{1.89\ \text{eV}} = 656\ \text{nm} \]

Answer: 1.89 eV, 656 nm, deep red: the H-alpha line (measured 656.3 nm) that colors glowing hydrogen clouds in astronomy photos.

The same fingerprints identify elements everywhere:

  • Neon signs: lines such as 640.2 nm (red) and 585.2 nm (yellow) mix to neon’s red-orange; other colors use other gases or phosphor coatings.
  • Sodium: the yellow pair at 589.0 and 589.6 nm colors old streetlights and salt sprinkled in a flame.
  • Fireworks: strontium salts burn red, barium green, copper blue.
  • Stars: helium was discovered in the Sun’s spectrum in 1868, 27 years before it was found on Earth.

5.4Continuous, emission and absorption spectra

Spread light into its colors and you will see one of three patterns (Figure 5.4):

  • Continuous: a hot, dense object (a filament, a red-hot stovetop, the Sun’s surface) glows at all wavelengths, because its crowded, jostling atoms offer energy gaps of every size. This is thermal radiation (Chapter 9).
  • Emission lines: a hot, thin gas of separate atoms emits only its own jumps: bright lines on black.
  • Absorption lines: a cooler gas in front of a continuous source removes exactly its own wavelengths. Thousands of these dark Fraunhofer lines cross the Sun’s spectrum, revealing its composition.

If the gas re-emits what it absorbs, why are the lines dark? Re-emission goes in random directions, so almost none of it continues along the beam toward you; the beam stays depleted at those wavelengths, while the cloud glows at them from the side.

5.5What happens to absorbed energy?

An absorbed photon no longer exists; its energy now belongs to the atom or molecule, and has three possible fates (Figure 5.5):

  • Re-emission: a new photon leaves after nanoseconds, in a random direction. This dominates in thin gases, where nothing disturbs the atom first.
  • Heat: in liquids and solids, neighbors jostle an excited atom far more often than once per nanosecond, so the energy usually drains into vibrations (temperature) before a photon can leave. That is why a black car gets hot in sunlight (Chapter 9).
  • Chemistry: the energy breaks or rearranges a bond: photosynthesis, vision, fading dyes, sunburn (Chapter 10).
Key Idea

Re-emission is not reflection. In reflection the wave shakes electrons that do not absorb it; they re-radiate in step with the wave and instantly, sending back one orderly beam at the mirror angle, same color. Re-emission follows a true absorption: delayed, random in direction and phase, sometimes a different color. Chapter 6 covers reflection.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: A mirror (or a window) works by absorbing each photon and then re-emitting it.

Reality: Absorb-and-re-emit would scatter light in random directions, so a mirror would look like frosted glass instead of showing your face. Reflection and transmission are coherent; the light is never absorbed. Light “slows down” in glass by in-step re-radiation too, not absorb-and-wait (Chapter 6).

5.6Fluorescence and phosphorescence

Molecules and crystals have vibrational sub-levels stacked on each electronic level. A molecule that absorbs an ultraviolet photon lands high on the excited state’s stack, slides to the bottom within picoseconds (shedding a little heat), then emits a visible photon on the way down. This is fluorescence: absorb short wavelengths, emit longer ones, within nanoseconds (Figure 5.6). The energy lost in between is the Stokes shift.

  • Highlighters glow under a black light (365 nm UV): the dye (often pyranine) turns invisible UV into yellow-green.
  • Fluorescent lamps: mercury vapor emits mostly 254 nm UV; a phosphor coating turns it into visible light.
  • White LEDs: a blue chip excites a yellow phosphor; blue plus yellow looks white (Section 5.9).

Sometimes the electron side-steps into a “stuck” state (a molecular triplet state, or a defect trap in a crystal) from which the drop is forbidden (Section 5.2). It leaks out slowly, so the material glows long after the light goes off: phosphorescence, as in glow-in-the-dark stars and exit signs.

Worked Example 5.3 — A highlighter’s energy budget

Given: a highlighter dye absorbs 365 nm black-light UV and fluoresces near 510 nm.

Find: the energy of each photon, and the fraction of each absorbed photon’s energy lost as heat.

Think: convert both wavelengths with Equation 5.2; the difference became vibration (heat).

\[ E_\text{in} = \frac{1240}{365} = 3.40\ \text{eV}, \qquad E_\text{out} = \frac{1240}{510} = 2.43\ \text{eV} \]

\[ \frac{E_\text{in} - E_\text{out}}{E_\text{in}} = \frac{0.97}{3.40} = 0.29 \]

Answer: 3.40 eV in, 2.43 eV out; about 29% becomes heat. In daylight the dye adds UV-converted light to what it reflects, which is why highlighter ink looks unnaturally bright.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Glow-in-the-dark materials must be radioactive.

Reality: Modern glow-in-the-dark products store energy from ordinary light in trapped electrons and release it slowly; that is why they fade and need recharging under a lamp. The myth has a real origin: early-1900s watch dials used radium paint, which glowed from radioactivity and badly harmed the workers who applied it.

5.7Molecules: vibrations (infrared) and rotations (microwaves)

Molecules can store energy in two more quantized ways (Figure 5.7):

  • Vibration: bonds stretch and bend like springs. Vibrational levels are spaced about 0.05–0.5 eV apart, matching infrared photons (about 2.5–25 µm).
  • Rotation: the whole molecule spins. Rotational levels are only about 0.0001–0.01 eV apart, matching microwave and far-infrared photons.

Absorption still needs an oscillating charge: a vibration absorbs infrared only if it makes the molecule’s charge swing back and forth (a changing dipole). Carbon dioxide’s bending vibration does, absorbing near 15 µm (0.083 eV), as does its asymmetric stretch near 4.3 µm. Nitrogen and oxygen, about 99% of air, are symmetric: stretching them shifts no charge, so they ignore infrared. That difference is the heart of the greenhouse effect (Chapter 9).

Rotations give sharp lines in gases; radio astronomers identify molecules in space by them. In liquid water, jammed molecules can’t rotate freely, so microwave absorption is broad; a microwave oven is not tuned to a special water resonance (Chapter 9). And because electronic jumps in molecules drag vibrations along, dyes and chlorophyll absorb broad bands of color (Chapter 7).

5.8Solids: energy bands and band gaps

Bring \(N\) atoms together into a crystal and their outer orbitals overlap. The Pauli exclusion principle forbids identical states, so each atomic level splits into \(N\) closely spaced levels, and with about \(10^{23}\) atoms these blur into continuous energy bands separated by band gaps (Figure 5.8). The highest filled band is the valence band; the next one up, empty at low temperature, is the conduction band. The gap between them, \(E_g\), controls almost everything.

  • Metals: the top band is only partly filled, so electrons can absorb any small energy. They move freely (conduction) and respond to all visible light, which makes metals shiny (Chapter 6).
  • Semiconductors: a modest gap (silicon 1.12 eV, GaAs 1.42 eV). Room-temperature thermal energy (\(k_BT \approx 0.026\) eV) lifts only a few electrons across, so they conduct a little.
  • Insulators: a wide gap (diamond about 5.5 eV, sapphire about 8.8 eV). Essentially no electrons cross, and visible photons can’t bridge it.
Analogy

A filled band is a completely full parking level: no car can move. Lift one car to the empty level above (the conduction band) and it can drive around, while the space it left (a “hole”) lets cars below shuffle too.

For light the rule is simple: a photon with energy at or above \(E_g\) can be absorbed (any energy above works, because the bands are continuous); a photon below \(E_g\) has nothing to excite and passes through (Table 5.1).

5.9LEDs and lasers

LEDs: light from a band gap

In a light-emitting diode (a junction between two differently doped regions of a semiconductor), an applied voltage drives conduction-band electrons to meet valence-band holes. Each drops across the gap and emits a photon:

\[ E_\text{photon} \approx E_g \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad \lambda \approx \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{E_g} \]

Engineers set the color by tuning the alloy: AlGaInP for red and amber, InGaN for green and blue. Silicon makes a poor LED: its “indirect” gap means a falling electron must also create a lattice vibration, so the energy usually becomes heat. White LEDs pair a blue InGaN chip (2014 Nobel Prize in Physics) with a yellow phosphor. And since each electron must be given at least \(E_g\), a red LED needs about 2 volts and a blue one about 3.

Worked Example 5.4 — Designing an LED

Given: GaAs has \(E_g = 1.42\) eV.

Find: (a) the wavelength a GaAs LED emits; (b) the band gap needed for a 450 nm blue LED.

Think: the photon energy is about the band gap (Equation 5.4).

(a) \(\lambda = 1240/1.42 = 873\ \text{nm}\): near-infrared, invisible to you.

(b) \(E_g = 1240/450 = 2.76\ \text{eV}\), well above silicon’s or GaAs’s gap: an InGaN alloy.

Answer: (a) 873 nm infrared, the kind of light GaAs-family LEDs send from a TV remote; (b) about 2.8 eV.

Lasers: copying photons

Einstein predicted in 1916–17 that a photon of exactly the right energy passing an excited atom can trigger it to emit: stimulated emission (Figure 5.1C). The new photon copies the trigger exactly (energy, direction, phase, polarization), so one photon becomes two, then four: a laser (light amplification by stimulated emission of radiation). It needs three ingredients:

  • Population inversion: more atoms in the upper level than the lower, or absorption wins. This never happens on its own, so the atoms are pumped (flash lamp, current, another laser), usually into a long-lived metastable level.
  • A cavity: mirrors bounce photons back and forth, copying them on every pass.
  • An exit: one mirror lets a small fraction leak out as the beam.

The first laser (Theodore Maiman, 1960) was a flash-lamp-pumped ruby crystal emitting at 694.3 nm. Diode lasers in barcode scanners and fiber-optic links are essentially LEDs with mirrors, and high-power infrared lasers cut, weld and 3D-print metal parts.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Laser light is made of especially powerful photons.

Reality: A laser’s photon energy is set by its transition like any other light; a red laser pointer’s photons each carry less energy than a blue LED’s. What is special is that the photons are identical and in step, so the beam stays narrow and focuses to a tiny, intense spot.

Chapter summary

  • One rule: \(hf = E_\text{upper} - E_\text{lower}\). Absorption is resonance: the frequency must match, whatever the atom’s size.
  • A photon is created during a downward jump, as the mixed-state cloud sloshes at \(f = \Delta E/h\). Re-emitted photons leave in random directions.
  • Hydrogen: \(\Delta E = 13.6\ \text{eV}(1/n_\text{lower}^2 - 1/n_\text{upper}^2)\); \(3 \to 2\) gives the 656 nm red line.
  • Hot dense objects: continuous spectra. Hot thin gases: emission lines. Cool gas before a hot source: dark lines at the same wavelengths.
  • Absorbed energy is re-emitted, turned into heat, or used for chemistry. Reflection is coherent and involves no absorption.
  • Fluorescence: absorb short, emit longer, within ns (Stokes shift). Phosphorescence: a stuck state releases light slowly.
  • Vibrations absorb infrared (≈ 0.05–0.5 eV), rotations microwaves (≈ 0.001 eV), if they move charge.
  • The band gap separates metals, semiconductors and insulators and sets an LED’s photon energy. Lasers amplify by stimulated emission from a population inversion.

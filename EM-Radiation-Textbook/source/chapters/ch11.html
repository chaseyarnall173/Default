Chapter 11

X-Rays: Shadows and Diffraction

The same invisible light that shows your dentist a cavity also reveals how the atoms in a solid are arranged. Both tricks work because an X-ray's wavelength is about the size of an atom.

The big picture

  • X-ray tubes make a continuous "braking" spectrum plus sharp characteristic lines that fingerprint the target metal.
  • An X-ray image is a shadow: absorption rises steeply with atomic number, so bone and metal block far more than flesh.
  • Crystal planes scatter X-rays in step only at angles satisfying Bragg's law, \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\).
  • Electrons have far shorter wavelengths than light, which lets electron microscopes see details light never can.

  • Derive and use Bragg's law.
  • Explain why electron microscopes can resolve much finer detail than light microscopes.

11.1Making X-rays: the X-ray tube

An X-ray tube is a vacuum tube with two electrodes (Figure 11.1). A hot filament, the cathode, boils off electrons. A high voltage \(V\) (about 20–150 kV) accelerates them toward a metal anode, or target. Each electron arrives with kinetic energy \(eV\), so 30 kV gives 30 keV electrons. When they slam into the target, about 99% of their energy becomes heat (which is why anodes rotate or are water-cooled) and about 1% comes out as X-rays.

11.2The X-ray spectrum: braking radiation and fingerprint lines

Two processes make the photons (Figure 11.2):

  • Bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation"). An electron swinging past a nucleus is sharply deflected. Accelerating charges radiate (Chapter 1), so it emits a photon. It can lose any fraction of its energy, so the spectrum is continuous, up to a hard limit where one electron gives all of its energy \(eV\) to one photon:
\[ \lambda_\text{min} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{eV} \qquad\text{e.g. } 35\ \text{kV} \Rightarrow 0.0354\ \text{nm} \]
  • Characteristic lines. An incoming electron knocks a K-shell (n = 1) electron out of a target atom. An electron from the L shell drops into the hole and emits a Kα photon; one from the M shell emits Kβ. These energies are set by the target atom's energy levels (Chapter 5), so they are a fingerprint of the element. They appear only when \(eV\) exceeds the K-shell binding energy (8.98 keV for copper).

Useful target lines: Cu Kα 0.1542 nm (8.04 keV), Co Kα 0.1790 nm, Mo Kα 0.0709 nm, W Kα 0.0209 nm (59 keV). Diffraction labs want one wavelength, so they filter out Kβ. For copper a thin nickel foil does it: nickel's K-edge (8.33 keV) lies between Cu Kα (8.05 keV) and Kβ (8.90 keV), so Kβ is absorbed and Kα passes.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — Moseley's law

Safe to skip. Treat the K-shell like hydrogen (Chapter 4) with nuclear charge \(Z-1\), since the one remaining K electron screens one proton. An L→K drop then gives \(E_{K\alpha} \approx 13.6\ \text{eV}\,(Z-1)^2(1 - \tfrac14) = 10.2\ \text{eV}\,(Z-1)^2\). For copper (Z = 29) that is 8.0 keV, against a measured 8.05 keV. In 1913 Henry Moseley used this pattern to show that the periodic table is ordered by atomic number, not atomic mass.

11.3X-ray imaging: shadows, not reflections

You Asked

“What does ‘X-ray vision’ mean — how do X-rays see through things?”

X-rays don't "see" anything: an X-ray image is a shadow. Photons pass straight through low-density, low-atomic-number material such as skin and muscle, bone and metal absorb them, and a detector records where they got through.

At medical energies (about 20–150 keV), the dominant absorption is photoelectric. Its probability rises very steeply with atomic number (roughly \(Z^3\) to \(Z^4\)) and falls steeply with energy (roughly \(1/E^3\)). Soft tissue is mostly H, C, N, and O (Z ≤ 8). Bone contains calcium (Z = 20) and phosphorus (Z = 15) and is nearly twice as dense. So at 30 keV, a centimeter of bone stops about 8 times more photons than a centimeter of muscle (Worked Example 11.1), and metal stops almost all of them. On a radiograph, bright means "few photons arrived," so bones look white. Comic-book X-ray vision, seeing X-rays bounced back off hidden objects, is not how it works (see the Myth box below).

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: "X-ray vision" means seeing X-rays that bounce off hidden objects.

Reality: Ordinary objects reflect almost no X-rays (for X-rays, a material's refractive index differs from 1 by only about \(10^{-5}\) or less), and nothing around you is lit by X-rays anyway. Real X-ray imaging always needs a source on one side and a detector on the other.

Worked Example 11.1 — Bone vs. muscle contrast

Given: At 30 keV, \(\mu/\rho\) = 0.379 cm²/g for soft tissue (\(\rho = 1.06\) g/cm³) and 1.331 cm²/g for cortical bone (\(\rho = 1.92\) g/cm³). At 100 keV: 0.169 and 0.186 cm²/g.

Find: The fraction transmitted through 1.0 cm of each, at both energies.

Think: \(T = e^{-\mu x}\) with \(\mu = (\mu/\rho)\rho\) (Eq. 10.4).

30 keV: tissue \(\mu = 0.402\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = e^{-0.402} = 0.67\). Bone \(\mu = 2.56\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = e^{-2.56} = 0.078\).

100 keV: tissue \(\mu = 0.179\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = 0.84\). Bone \(\mu = 0.356\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = 0.70\).

Answer: At 30 keV, 67% vs. 8% get through, so the contrast is 8.6:1. At 100 keV it is 84% vs. 70%, only 1.2:1. Lower energy gives more contrast but more absorbed dose, so radiologists pick tube voltage to balance the two.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: An X-ray machine makes you or the room radioactive.

Reality: X-ray photons are absorbed or pass through, and they are gone the moment the tube switches off. Medical X-ray energies are far too low to change nuclei, so nothing is left behind.

Contrast agents and CT

Soft organs look alike on an X-ray, so doctors add high-Z contrast agents, such as iodine (Z = 53) in blood vessels or barium (Z = 56) in the gut. Their K-edges (33 and 37 keV) sit right in the diagnostic range.

A single radiograph squashes 3D anatomy into one shadow. Computed tomography (CT) rotates the tube and detectors around the body, records hundreds of projections, and lets a computer reconstruct a map of \(\mu\) slice by slice. The values are reported in Hounsfield units (air −1000, water 0, dense bone around +1000 or higher). The price is dose: about 7.7 mSv for an abdominal CT versus 0.1 mSv for a chest X-ray (Table 10.1). Engineers use the same method, industrial CT, to find porosity in castings, voids in welds, and defects in 3D-printed parts without cutting them open.

11.4Why X-rays can "see" atoms

Atoms in a solid sit about 0.1–0.4 nm apart. Visible light (400–700 nm) is thousands of times too long to resolve that (Chapter 8). X-ray wavelengths of 0.05–0.25 nm match it. When an X-ray passes through a crystal, its electric field shakes every atom's electrons, and each shaken electron re-radiates a weak spherical wavelet (Chapter 6). A crystal is a 3D array of these sources, a natural diffraction grating. The wavelets cancel almost everywhere and add up only in a few special directions.

Key Idea

Diffraction needs a wavelength comparable to the spacing being probed (Chapter 8). Atoms are about 0.1–0.4 nm apart, so the probe must be X-rays (or electrons or neutrons) with wavelengths of a fraction of a nanometer.

11.5Bragg's law

W. L. Bragg found the simplest way to predict those directions (1913). Treat the crystal as stacks of parallel atomic planes a distance \(d\) apart. A ray scattered by the second plane travels farther than one scattered by the first, by \(d\sin\theta\) on the way in plus \(d\sin\theta\) on the way out (Figure 11.5). The scattered waves add in step when that extra path is a whole number of wavelengths:

\[ n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta \]

where \(\theta\) is measured from the planes (not from the normal), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) is the order, and \(\lambda\) is the X-ray wavelength.

Key Idea

A Bragg "reflection" is not a mirror bouncing light. It is interference: the planes scatter X-rays at every angle, but the scattered waves add up only at angles where \(2d\sin\theta = n\lambda\). A diffractometer swings its detector through the angle \(2\theta\) between the incoming and outgoing beams, so patterns are plotted against \(2\theta\).

Analogy

Clap in front of a tall stone staircase and the echo comes back as a chirp: each step returns a little echo, spaced in time by the step depth. Crystal planes do the same with X-rays, except the "pitch" shows up as an angle.

11.6Electron microscopes and electron diffraction

Electrons are waves too, with \(\lambda = h/p\) (Chapter 8). Accelerate one through 100 kV and, including a small relativistic correction,

\[ \lambda_e = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV\left(1+\dfrac{eV}{2m_ec^2}\right)}} = 0.0037\ \text{nm} \quad (100\ \text{kV}) \]

That is about 40 times shorter than Cu Kα, so electron Bragg angles are tiny. For atomic planes 0.21 nm apart, \(\sin\theta = 0.0037/(2 \times 0.21)\), so \(\theta \approx 0.5^\circ\). Electrons interact with matter far more strongly than X-rays, so samples must be very thin (under about 100 nm) and held in vacuum.

  • TEM (transmission electron microscope): shines electrons through a thin foil. It can image defects and even columns of atoms, and it records electron diffraction patterns from regions a few nanometers across.
  • SEM (scanning electron microscope): scans a focused beam over a surface and collects knocked-out electrons to image surfaces in fine detail.
  • EDS: the beam knocks out inner-shell electrons, just as in an X-ray tube, so the sample emits its own characteristic X-rays. Their energies (Section 11.2) identify the elements present.

Chapter summary

  • X-ray tubes: bremsstrahlung gives a continuum down to \(\lambda_\text{min} = hc/eV\); K-shell vacancies give element-specific Kα/Kβ lines (Cu Kα = 0.1542 nm).
  • X-ray images are shadows: photoelectric absorption scales roughly as \(Z^3\text{–}Z^4/E^3\), so bone and metal absorb far more than soft tissue. CT maps μ in 3D.
  • Bragg's law \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\), with θ from the planes; patterns are plotted against 2θ.
  • 100 kV electrons have λ ≈ 0.0037 nm, enabling electron microscopes (TEM, SEM), electron diffraction, and EDS.

Chapter 11

X-Rays and Materials Science

The same invisible light that shows your dentist a cavity also told us how the atoms in a copper wire are stacked. Both tricks work because an X-ray's wavelength is about the size of an atom.

The big picture

  • X-ray tubes make a continuous "braking" spectrum plus sharp characteristic lines that fingerprint the target metal.
  • An X-ray image is a shadow: absorption rises steeply with atomic number, so bone and metal block far more than flesh.
  • Crystal planes scatter X-rays in step only at angles satisfying Bragg's law, \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\).
  • Peak positions give d-spacings, which give the lattice parameter and the crystal structure (FCC vs. BCC) through selection rules.

  • Derive and use Bragg's law and \(d_{hkl} = a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}\).
  • Relate (110) and (220), and explain why (100) is missing for BCC and FCC.
  • Connect interplanar spacing with planar density, and index a powder pattern to find the structure and lattice parameter.

11.1Making X-rays: the X-ray tube

An X-ray tube is a vacuum tube with two electrodes (Figure 11.1). A hot filament, the cathode, boils off electrons. A high voltage \(V\) (about 20–150 kV) accelerates them toward a metal anode, or target. Each electron arrives with kinetic energy \(eV\), so 30 kV gives 30 keV electrons. When they slam into the target, about 99% of their energy becomes heat (which is why anodes rotate or are water-cooled) and about 1% comes out as X-rays.

11.2The X-ray spectrum: braking radiation and fingerprint lines

Two processes make the photons (Figure 11.2):

  • Bremsstrahlung ("braking radiation"). An electron swinging past a nucleus is sharply deflected. Accelerating charges radiate (Chapter 1), so it emits a photon. It can lose any fraction of its energy, so the spectrum is continuous, up to a hard limit where one electron gives all of its energy \(eV\) to one photon:
\[ \lambda_\text{min} = \frac{hc}{eV} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{eV} \qquad\text{e.g. } 35\ \text{kV} \Rightarrow 0.0354\ \text{nm} \]
  • Characteristic lines. An incoming electron knocks a K-shell (n = 1) electron out of a target atom. An electron from the L shell drops into the hole and emits a Kα photon; one from the M shell emits Kβ. These energies are set by the target atom's energy levels (Chapter 5), so they are a fingerprint of the element. They appear only when \(eV\) exceeds the K-shell binding energy (8.98 keV for copper).

Useful target lines: Cu Kα 0.1542 nm (8.04 keV), Co Kα 0.1790 nm, Mo Kα 0.0709 nm, W Kα 0.0209 nm (59 keV). Diffraction labs want one wavelength, so they filter out Kβ. For copper a thin nickel foil does it: nickel's K-edge (8.33 keV) lies between Cu Kα (8.05 keV) and Kβ (8.90 keV), so Kβ is absorbed and Kα passes.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — Moseley's law

Safe to skip. Treat the K-shell like hydrogen (Chapter 4) with nuclear charge \(Z-1\), since the one remaining K electron screens one proton. An L→K drop then gives \(E_{K\alpha} \approx 13.6\ \text{eV}\,(Z-1)^2(1 - \tfrac14) = 10.2\ \text{eV}\,(Z-1)^2\). For copper (Z = 29) that is 8.0 keV, against a measured 8.05 keV. In 1913 Henry Moseley used this pattern to show that the periodic table is ordered by atomic number, not atomic mass.

11.3X-ray imaging: shadows, not reflections

You Asked

“What does ‘X-ray vision’ mean — how do X-rays see through things?”

X-rays don't "see" anything: an X-ray image is a shadow. Photons pass straight through low-density, low-atomic-number material such as skin and muscle, bone and metal absorb them, and a detector records where they got through.

At medical energies (about 20–150 keV), the dominant absorption is photoelectric. Its probability rises very steeply with atomic number (roughly \(Z^3\) to \(Z^4\)) and falls steeply with energy (roughly \(1/E^3\)). Soft tissue is mostly H, C, N, and O (Z ≤ 8). Bone contains calcium (Z = 20) and phosphorus (Z = 15) and is nearly twice as dense. So at 30 keV, a centimeter of bone stops about 8 times more photons than a centimeter of muscle (Worked Example 11.1), and metal stops almost all of them. On a radiograph, bright means "few photons arrived," so bones look white. Comic-book X-ray vision, seeing X-rays bounced back off hidden objects, is not how it works (see the Myth box below).

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: "X-ray vision" means seeing X-rays that bounce off hidden objects.

Reality: Ordinary objects reflect almost no X-rays (for X-rays, a material's refractive index differs from 1 by only about \(10^{-5}\) or less), and nothing around you is lit by X-rays anyway. Real X-ray imaging always needs a source on one side and a detector on the other.

Worked Example 11.1 — Bone vs. muscle contrast

Given: At 30 keV, \(\mu/\rho\) = 0.379 cm²/g for soft tissue (\(\rho = 1.06\) g/cm³) and 1.331 cm²/g for cortical bone (\(\rho = 1.92\) g/cm³). At 100 keV: 0.169 and 0.186 cm²/g.

Find: The fraction transmitted through 1.0 cm of each, at both energies.

Think: \(T = e^{-\mu x}\) with \(\mu = (\mu/\rho)\rho\) (Eq. 10.4).

30 keV: tissue \(\mu = 0.402\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = e^{-0.402} = 0.67\). Bone \(\mu = 2.56\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = e^{-2.56} = 0.078\).

100 keV: tissue \(\mu = 0.179\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = 0.84\). Bone \(\mu = 0.356\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), \(T = 0.70\).

Answer: At 30 keV, 67% vs. 8% get through, so the contrast is 8.6:1. At 100 keV it is 84% vs. 70%, only 1.2:1. Lower energy gives more contrast but more absorbed dose, so radiologists pick tube voltage to balance the two.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: An X-ray machine makes you or the room radioactive.

Reality: X-ray photons are absorbed or pass through, and they are gone the moment the tube switches off. Medical X-ray energies are far too low to change nuclei, so nothing is left behind.

Contrast agents and CT

Soft organs look alike on an X-ray, so doctors add high-Z contrast agents, such as iodine (Z = 53) in blood vessels or barium (Z = 56) in the gut. Their K-edges (33 and 37 keV) sit right in the diagnostic range.

A single radiograph squashes 3D anatomy into one shadow. Computed tomography (CT) rotates the tube and detectors around the body, records hundreds of projections, and lets a computer reconstruct a map of \(\mu\) slice by slice. The values are reported in Hounsfield units (air −1000, water 0, dense bone around +1000 or higher). The price is dose: about 7.7 mSv for an abdominal CT versus 0.1 mSv for a chest X-ray (Table 10.1). Engineers use the same method, industrial CT, to find porosity in castings, voids in welds, and defects in 3D-printed parts without cutting them open.

11.4Why X-rays can "see" atoms

Atoms in a solid sit about 0.1–0.4 nm apart. Visible light (400–700 nm) is thousands of times too long to resolve that (Chapter 8). X-ray wavelengths of 0.05–0.25 nm match it. When an X-ray passes through a crystal, its electric field shakes every atom's electrons, and each shaken electron re-radiates a weak spherical wavelet (Chapter 6). A crystal is a 3D array of these sources, a natural diffraction grating. The wavelets cancel almost everywhere and add up only in a few special directions.

Key Idea

Diffraction needs a wavelength comparable to the spacing being probed (Chapter 8). Atoms are about 0.1–0.4 nm apart, so the probe must be X-rays (or electrons or neutrons) with wavelengths of a fraction of a nanometer.

11.5Bragg's law

W. L. Bragg found the simplest way to predict those directions (1913). Treat the crystal as stacks of parallel atomic planes a distance \(d\) apart. A ray scattered by the second plane travels farther than one scattered by the first, by \(d\sin\theta\) on the way in plus \(d\sin\theta\) on the way out (Figure 11.5). The scattered waves add in step when that extra path is a whole number of wavelengths:

\[ n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta \]

where \(\theta\) is measured from the planes (not from the normal), \(n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots\) is the order, and \(\lambda\) is the X-ray wavelength.

Key Idea

A Bragg "reflection" is not a mirror bouncing light. It is interference: the planes scatter X-rays at every angle, but the scattered waves add up only at angles where \(2d\sin\theta = n\lambda\). A diffractometer swings its detector through the angle \(2\theta\) between the incoming and outgoing beams, so patterns are plotted against \(2\theta\).

Analogy

Clap in front of a tall stone staircase and the echo comes back as a chirp: each step returns a little echo, spaced in time by the step depth. Crystal planes do the same with X-rays, except the "pitch" shows up as an angle.

11.6Planes, Miller indices, and d-spacings

For a cubic crystal, the spacing between adjacent \((hkl)\) planes is

\[ d_{hkl} = \frac{a}{\sqrt{h^{2}+k^{2}+l^{2}}} \]

So \(d_{100} = a\), \(d_{110} = a/\sqrt2\), \(d_{111} = a/\sqrt3\), \(d_{200} = a/2\), \(d_{220} = a/\sqrt8\). Larger indices mean more closely spaced planes.

Materials Science Connection

Miller indices, the ME2340 version. For a plane: take its intercepts with x, y, z in units of the lattice parameter \(a\), take reciprocals, clear fractions, and write \((hkl)\), with a bar for negatives. A direction \([uvw]\) is the vector components, reduced to smallest integers. Braces \(\{hkl\}\) and angle brackets \(\langle uvw\rangle\) denote whole families. In cubic crystals, \([hkl]\) is perpendicular to \((hkl)\).

Lattice parameter from atomic radius. Atoms touch along the face diagonal in FCC (\(4R = a\sqrt2\), so \(a = 2R\sqrt2\)) and along the body diagonal in BCC (\(4R = a\sqrt3\), so \(a = 4R/\sqrt3\)). Copper (FCC, R = 0.1278 nm) has \(a = 0.3615\) nm; α-iron (BCC, R = 0.1241 nm) has \(a = 0.2866\) nm. An X-ray pattern measures \(a\) directly, which is how those radii were found in the first place.

You Asked

“How can parallel planes have different Miller indices — what's the deal with (110) vs (220), or (100) vs (200)?”

(220) planes have the same orientation as (110) planes but are packed twice as closely: \(d_{220} = d_{110}/2\). The (220) set includes every (110) plane plus an extra plane halfway between each pair (Figure 11.6a).

Geometrically, a (220) plane cuts the axes at \(a/2\), \(a/2\), \(\infty\) instead of \(a\), \(a\), \(\infty\). In plain geometry you would reduce (220) to (110). X-ray crystallographers keep the extra factor deliberately, because it matches Bragg's law: \(2\lambda = 2d_{110}\sin\theta\) is the same equation as \(\lambda = 2(d_{110}/2)\sin\theta = 2d_{220}\sin\theta\). A "(220) reflection" is the second-order (\(n = 2\)) reflection from the (110) planes. Labeling it (220) lets us always write Bragg's law with \(n = 1\).

The (100)/(200) case adds a twist. In BCC, the body-center atoms lie on planes exactly halfway between the (100) planes of corner atoms (Figure 11.6b). At the angle where corner planes scatter in step (path difference λ), the halfway planes are off by λ/2. They hold the same number of atoms, so they cancel the corner planes exactly, and no (100) peak appears. At the (200) angle, all planes, spaced \(a/2\), are in step. FCC behaves the same way because its face-center atoms also sit on the halfway planes. That is why BCC and FCC patterns list (200), never (100).

Safe to skip. Add the wave from every atom \(j\) in the unit cell, at fractional position \((x_j, y_j, z_j)\), with phase \(2\pi(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)\): \(F_{hkl} = \sum_j f_j\, e^{2\pi i(hx_j+ky_j+lz_j)}\). Intensity goes as \(|F|^2\), and Table 11.1 falls right out.

11.7Interplanar spacing and planar density

You Asked

“If the interplanar spacing is higher, does that mean the density is higher?”

Within one crystal, yes for the planar density: the families of planes with the largest spacing are the most densely packed planes. It says nothing about the material's mass density (g/cm³), which is the same however you slice it.

The reason is bookkeeping. Every atom lies on some plane of a given family, and the number of atoms per unit volume \(n_v\) is fixed. Stack planes with \(PD\) atoms per unit area at spacing \(d\), and a slab of area \(A\) and thickness \(L\) holds \(PD \cdot A \cdot (L/d)\) atoms. Setting that equal to \(n_v A L\):

\[ PD = n_v\, d \]

So widely spaced planes must be crowded and closely spaced planes sparse. Picture slicing a loaf into thick slices or thin ones: same bread, different amount per slice. For FCC, (111) has the largest spacing (\(a/\sqrt3\)) and is the close-packed plane; for BCC, (110) is both. Figure 11.7 and Worked Example 11.2 check this for copper.

Two caveats. First, use the distance between adjacent atom-bearing planes. For FCC (100) that is \(a/2\) (\(d_{200}\)), because face-center atoms sit halfway. Second, the relation is exact for crystals with one atom per lattice point (FCC and BCC metals). In crystals with several atoms per lattice point (NaCl, diamond, HCP), the planes in a family may not be identical or evenly spaced, so it becomes an approximation.

Worked Example 11.2 — Planar density vs. spacing in copper

Given: Copper is FCC with \(R = 0.1278\) nm.

Find: Planar density \(PD\) and adjacent-plane spacing \(d\) for (100), (110), (111), and check that \(PD/d = n_v\).

Think: \(a = 2R\sqrt2\). Count atoms centered on each plane section (Callister style), divide by its area, then compare with \(n_v = 4/a^3\).

\(a = 2(0.1278)\sqrt2 = 0.3615\) nm, so \(n_v = 4/a^3 = 84.7\ \text{atoms/nm}^3\).

(100): 2 atoms on \(a^2\): \(PD = 2/a^2 = 15.3\ \text{nm}^{-2}\); \(d = a/2 = 0.181\) nm; \(PD/d = 84.7\).

(110): 2 atoms on \(a \times a\sqrt2\): \(PD = 10.8\ \text{nm}^{-2}\); \(d = a/(2\sqrt2) = 0.128\) nm; \(PD/d = 84.7\).

(111): 2 atoms on a triangle of area \((\sqrt3/2)a^2\): \(PD = 17.7\ \text{nm}^{-2}\); \(d = a/\sqrt3 = 0.209\) nm; \(PD/d = 84.7\).

Answer: (111) has the largest d (0.209 nm) and the highest PD (17.7 atoms/nm²); (110) has the smallest of both. All three give \(PD/d = n_v = 84.7\) atoms/nm³.

Materials Science Connection

This is why metals slip on their densest planes. Close-packed planes are the farthest apart and the smoothest, so they slide past each other most easily. FCC metals slip on {111} planes and BCC metals mostly on {110}, which you'll see as "slip systems" in the dislocations chapter of Callister. In a diffraction pattern, those same planes usually give the first strong peak, (111) for FCC and (110) for BCC, because the largest d gives the smallest angle.

11.8Reading a diffraction pattern

In powder diffraction, the sample contains millions of randomly oriented grains, so every plane family finds some grains at its Bragg angle. The detector sweeps \(2\theta\) and records a peak for each allowed family (Figure 11.8).

Peak positions are only the start. A pattern also reveals:

  • Phase identification: each crystalline compound has a fingerprint pattern. One example is telling FCC austenite from BCC ferrite in a steel.
  • Composition and strain: alloying or residual stress shifts \(a\) slightly, so peaks shift.
  • Grain size: very small crystallites (below about 100 nm) broaden the peaks.
  • Amorphous material: glass and many polymers give broad humps instead of sharp peaks, because they have no long-range order.
Worked Example 11.3 — BCC iron, (220) planes (Callister-style)

Given: α-iron is BCC with \(a = 0.2866\) nm. Monochromatic X-rays with \(\lambda = 0.1790\) nm (Co Kα); first-order reflection (\(n = 1\)) from (220) planes.

Find: \(d_{220}\) and the diffraction angle \(2\theta\).

Think: Eq. 11.3 for d, then Bragg's law for θ. (220) is allowed for BCC since 2 + 2 + 0 is even.

\[ d_{220} = \frac{0.2866\ \text{nm}}{\sqrt{2^2+2^2+0^2}} = \frac{0.2866}{2.828} = 0.1013\ \text{nm} \]

\[ \sin\theta = \frac{n\lambda}{2d} = \frac{0.1790}{2(0.1013)} = 0.8833 \;\Rightarrow\; \theta = 62.04^\circ,\quad 2\theta = 124.08^\circ \]

Answer: \(d_{220} = 0.1013\) nm and \(2\theta \approx 124.1^\circ\). (Callister's printed 124.26° comes from rounding \(\sin\theta\) to 0.884 before taking the arcsine. Carry four digits and you get 124.08°.)

Worked Example 11.4 — Identify an unknown cubic metal

Given: Cu Kα (\(\lambda = 0.1542\) nm) peaks at \(2\theta\) = 38.5°, 44.7°, 65.1°, 78.2°, 82.4°.

Find: The structure and lattice parameter.

Think: Bragg plus Eq. 11.3 give \(\sin^2\theta = (\lambda^2/4a^2)\,N\), so the \(\sin^2\theta\) values must be in the ratio of the allowed N values in Table 11.1.

  • \(\sin^2\theta\): 0.1087, 0.1446, 0.2895, 0.3978, 0.4339
  • Divided by the first: 1, 1.330, 2.663, 3.659, 3.992. These are not integers, so try ×2 (BCC) or ×3 (FCC).
  • ×3: 3.00, 3.99, 7.99, 10.98, 11.97 → N = 3, 4, 8, 11, 12. That is FCC: (111), (200), (220), (311), (222).
  • \(a = \lambda\sqrt{N}/(2\sin\theta)\): from (111), \(0.1542\sqrt3/(2 \times 0.3297) = 0.405\) nm. The other peaks agree within 0.1%.

Answer: FCC with \(a \approx 0.405\) nm, which matches aluminum (\(a = 0.4049\) nm).

11.9Electron microscopes and electron diffraction

Electrons are waves too, with \(\lambda = h/p\) (Chapter 8). Accelerate one through 100 kV and, including a small relativistic correction,

\[ \lambda_e = \frac{h}{\sqrt{2m_e eV\left(1+\dfrac{eV}{2m_ec^2}\right)}} = 0.0037\ \text{nm} \quad (100\ \text{kV}) \]

That is about 40 times shorter than Cu Kα, so electron Bragg angles are tiny. For copper's (111) planes, \(\sin\theta = 0.0037/(2 \times 0.2087)\), so \(\theta \approx 0.5^\circ\). Electrons interact with matter far more strongly than X-rays, so samples must be very thin (under about 100 nm) and held in vacuum.

  • TEM (transmission electron microscope): shines electrons through a thin foil. It can image dislocations and even columns of atoms, and it records electron diffraction patterns from regions a few nanometers across.
  • SEM (scanning electron microscope): scans a focused beam over a surface and collects knocked-out electrons to image fracture surfaces and microstructure.
  • EDS: the beam knocks out inner-shell electrons, just as in an X-ray tube, so the sample emits its own characteristic X-rays. Their energies (Section 11.2) identify the elements present.

Chapter summary

  • X-ray tubes: bremsstrahlung gives a continuum down to \(\lambda_\text{min} = hc/eV\); K-shell vacancies give element-specific Kα/Kβ lines (Cu Kα = 0.1542 nm).
  • X-ray images are shadows: photoelectric absorption scales roughly as \(Z^3\text{–}Z^4/E^3\), so bone and metal absorb far more than soft tissue. CT maps μ in 3D.
  • Bragg's law \(n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta\), with θ from the planes; patterns are plotted against 2θ.
  • Cubic spacing: \(d_{hkl} = a/\sqrt{h^2+k^2+l^2}\). (220) planes are parallel to (110) at half the spacing, and a (220) reflection is the second order of (110).
  • BCC needs \(h+k+l\) even; FCC needs all odd or all even. (100) vanishes in both because halfway-plane atoms scatter exactly out of step.
  • Planar density = \(n_v \times d\): the widest-spaced planes are the densest (FCC {111}, BCC {110}), and they are also the slip planes.
  • 100 kV electrons have λ ≈ 0.0037 nm, enabling TEM, electron diffraction, and EDS.

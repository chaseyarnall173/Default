Chapter 10

Ionizing Radiation, Shielding, and Space

Your Wi-Fi router and a dental X-ray machine both emit "radiation." Only one of them can break a DNA strand, and the reason comes down to one number per photon.

The big picture

  • "Ionizing" means one photon or particle carries enough energy (about 10 eV) to knock an electron out of an atom.
  • Damage means broken bonds in DNA, made directly or through free radicals.
  • Dose is measured in gray (energy per kilogram) and sievert (gray weighted for biological effect).
  • Distance and mass are the shields. Magnetic fields steer charged particles but do nothing to photons or neutrons.

After this chapter you can

  • Decide whether a photon is ionizing from its wavelength or frequency.
  • Compare how far alpha, beta, gamma, and neutron radiation go, and what stops each.
  • Read doses in mSv and put everyday exposures on one scale.
  • Use the inverse-square law, exponential attenuation, and half-value layers.
  • Compute the spiral radius of a charged particle in a magnetic field, and explain what a magnetosphere does and does not block.

10.1Two meanings of "radiation"

Radiation means energy traveling outward from a source. It comes in two kinds:

  • Electromagnetic radiation: photons, from radio waves to gamma rays (Chapter 3).
  • Particle radiation: fast particles with mass, such as alpha particles, electrons, neutrons, and the protons and nuclei of cosmic rays.

Either kind is ionizing radiation only if a single photon or particle carries enough energy to rip electrons off atoms.

Key Idea

What matters is the energy per packet, not the total energy. A beam of a trillion weak photons can warm you, but it cannot ionize. One energetic photon can.

10.2The ionizing threshold

To ionize an atom, you have to supply at least its ionization energy (Chapter 4). For atoms this ranges from 3.9 eV (cesium) to 24.6 eV (helium); water, most of your body, needs 12.6 eV. So the conventional dividing line is about 10 eV, which corresponds to a wavelength of

\[ \lambda = \frac{hc}{E} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{10\ \text{eV}} = 124\ \text{nm}, \]

deep in the ultraviolet. Different agencies put the line anywhere from 10 to 33 eV, so treat it as a fuzzy boundary (Figure 10.1).

Why can't a brighter lamp make up the difference? An electron absorbs one photon at a time (Chapter 2), and it sheds a 2 eV kick as heat long before a second photon arrives. Brightness changes how many electrons get kicked, not how hard each kick is.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: A powerful enough Wi-Fi router or cell tower could ionize your cells.

Reality: A 2.4 GHz photon carries about \(10^{-5}\) eV, a million times too little. Strong microwaves can heat tissue (a microwave oven), which is a different mechanism with a different safety question.

Worked Example 10.1 — Which photons can ionize?

Given: Wi-Fi at \(f = 2.4\) GHz, UV-B at \(\lambda = 300\) nm, a 60 keV dental X-ray photon.

Find: Each photon's energy, and whether it can ionize.

Think: Compare \(E = hf = hc/\lambda\) with the ~10 eV line.

\[ E_\text{Wi-Fi} = \frac{(6.626\times10^{-34})(2.4\times10^{9})}{1.602\times10^{-19}}\ \text{eV} = 9.9\times10^{-6}\ \text{eV} \]

\[ E_\text{UV-B} = \frac{1240\ \text{eV}\cdot\text{nm}}{300\ \text{nm}} = 4.1\ \text{eV} \]

The X-ray photon is 60,000 eV. As it slows, it frees an electron that knocks loose many more; one ion pair costs about 34 eV on average, so one photon creates roughly \(60{,}000/34 \approx 1800\) ions.

Answer: Wi-Fi \(\approx 10^{-5}\) eV (no); UV-B 4.1 eV (no, but see Section 10.3); X-ray 60 keV (yes, about 1800 ionizations).

10.3UV-B and sunburn: damage below the line

UV-B (280–315 nm, 3.9–4.4 eV) is not ionizing, yet it gives you sunburn and skin cancer. The reason is photochemistry: a photon does not need to remove an electron to cause trouble; it only needs to be absorbed by a molecule and rearrange its bonds.

DNA bases absorb UV strongly. A UV-B photon absorbed where two thymine bases sit side by side can fuse them into a thymine dimer (Figure 10.3c), a kink that stalls DNA copying. Cells sense it and trigger inflammation (the redness); badly damaged cells self-destruct (the peeling). Dimers repaired wrongly become mutations.

Key Idea

"Non-ionizing" does not mean "harmless." Visible and IR photons are too weak to break most bonds, but UV photons (3–10 eV) are in the bond-breaking range even though they cannot ionize.

10.4The particle zoo: alpha, beta, gamma, neutrons, cosmic rays

Unstable nuclei, reactors, and space produce five kinds of ionizing radiation:

  • Alpha (α): a helium nucleus (charge +2), typically about 5 MeV. It ionizes densely and stops in a few cm of air or in the dead outer layer of skin, so it is dangerous mainly when inhaled or swallowed (radon gas).
  • Beta (β): an electron (or positron) shot out of a nucleus. It travels a few millimeters in tissue and is stopped by a few millimeters of aluminum or plastic.
  • Gamma (γ) and X-rays: high-energy photons; the name records the source (nucleus vs. electrons). Very penetrating; lead or thick concrete cuts them down.
  • Neutrons (n): uncharged, so they ignore electrons and hit only nuclei. They pass through lead, are slowed by hydrogen-rich materials, and can make materials radioactive.
  • Cosmic rays: nuclei from deep space near light speed, about 85% protons, 14% helium, and 1% heavier "HZE" nuclei such as iron. The Sun adds bursts of protons during storms.
Key Idea

A charged particle tugs on every electron it passes, so it loses energy steadily and stops at a definite range. A photon or neutron travels untouched until one chance collision, so photons fall off exponentially (Section 10.8) instead of stopping at a wall.

Analogy

An alpha particle is a bowling ball rolled down a crowded hallway: it hits everyone and stops fast. A gamma ray is a single pellet fired down the same hallway: it usually flies through, but now and then it hits someone hard.

10.5How radiation damages cells

Radiation harms a cell by breaking chemical bonds, and the bonds that matter most are in DNA. Damage happens in two ways (Figure 10.3):

  • Direct: the track ionizes the DNA molecule itself and breaks a strand.
  • Indirect: the track ionizes water, most of a cell, making free radicals such as hydroxyl (•OH) that drift a few nanometers and attack DNA. For X-rays and gamma rays, most damage is indirect.

Cells fix single-strand breaks easily, copying from the other strand. Double-strand breaks are harder, and tight clusters of breaks hardest; dense alpha and heavy-nucleus tracks make exactly those clusters, so they do more harm per joule. The outcome is repair, cell death, or a mutation that may raise cancer risk years later. Rapidly dividing cells (bone marrow, gut lining, tumors) are the most sensitive.

10.6Measuring dose: gray and sievert

Absorbed dose \(D\) is energy deposited per kilogram of tissue, measured in gray: 1 Gy = 1 J/kg. Because a joule from alpha particles does more harm than a joule from X-rays, we also use the equivalent dose \(H\), in sieverts (Sv):

\[ H = w_R\, D \]

where the radiation weighting factor \(w_R\) is 1 for photons and electrons, 2 for protons, 20 for alpha particles and heavy nuclei, and 2.5–20 for neutrons depending on energy. Whole-body risk uses the effective dose, also in Sv, which further weights each organ's sensitivity. Older US units: 1 Gy = 100 rad, 1 Sv = 100 rem.

Key Idea

A gray is a tiny amount of heat: 1 J/kg warms water by 0.00024 °C. A sudden whole-body 4–5 Sv kills half of those exposed, yet warms the body by about a thousandth of a degree. What matters is where the energy goes (DNA bonds), not how much.

10.7Distance: the inverse-square law

A small source sends radiation out evenly in all directions. At distance \(r\) the same energy is spread over a sphere of area \(4\pi r^2\), so the intensity is

\[ I = \frac{P}{4\pi r^{2}} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{I_2}{I_1} = \left(\frac{r_1}{r_2}\right)^{2} \]

Valid for a point-like source with nothing absorbing in between. Close to a large source (a big tank, the ground), dose falls off more slowly.

Worked Example 10.2 — Stepping back from a source

Given: A small lab source gives a dose rate of 20 µSv/h at 0.50 m.

Find: (a) The rate at 2.0 m. (b) The distance where it drops to 0.50 µSv/h.

Think: Point source, air barely absorbs gamma rays over meters, so use Eq. 10.3.

(a) \( \dot{H}_2 = 20\ \mu\text{Sv/h} \times (0.50/2.0)^{2} = 1.25\ \mu\text{Sv/h} \)

(b) \( r_2 = r_1\sqrt{\dot{H}_1/\dot{H}_2} = 0.50\ \text{m}\times\sqrt{20/0.50} = 0.50 \times 6.32 = 3.2\ \text{m} \)

Answer: (a) 1.25 µSv/h, a factor of 16 lower for 4× the distance. (b) about 3.2 m.

10.8Shielding: why mass, and why hydrogen

Photons: exponential attenuation

Each photon has a fixed chance per centimeter of interacting, so every layer removes the same fraction of whatever arrives:

\[ I = I_0\, e^{-\mu x}, \qquad \mu = \left(\frac{\mu}{\rho}\right)\rho \]

where \(\mu\) is the linear attenuation coefficient (cm⁻¹), \(\mu/\rho\) the mass attenuation coefficient (cm²/g, tabulated by NIST), \(\rho\) the density, and \(x\) the thickness. This "narrow-beam" law slightly underestimates real transmission, since scattered photons can still reach you.

The thickness that halves the beam is the half-value layer:

\[ \text{HVL} = \frac{\ln 2}{\mu} \approx \frac{0.693}{\mu} \]
Worked Example 10.3 — Lead vs. water against 1 MeV gamma rays

Given: NIST mass attenuation coefficients at 1.0 MeV: lead 0.0710 cm²/g (\(\rho = 11.35\) g/cm³), water 0.0707 cm²/g (\(\rho = 1.00\) g/cm³).

Find: The HVL of each, and the thickness that cuts the beam to 1%.

Think: Eq. 10.4 with \(I/I_0 = 0.01\) gives \(x = \ln(100)/\mu\).

Lead: \(\mu = 0.0710 \times 11.35 = 0.806\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), so HVL \(= 0.693/0.806 = 0.86\) cm, and \(x_{1\%} = 4.61/0.806 = 5.7\) cm.

Water: \(\mu = 0.0707\ \text{cm}^{-1}\), so HVL \(= 9.8\) cm, and \(x_{1\%} = 65\) cm.

Mass per area: lead \(5.7 \times 11.35 \approx 65\ \text{g/cm}^2\); water \(65 \times 1.00 = 65\ \text{g/cm}^2\).

Answer: HVL 0.86 cm (lead) vs. 9.8 cm (water); 1% needs 5.7 cm of lead or 65 cm of water, which is the same mass per area. Lead wins on space, not on weight.

At lower energies (medical X-rays, tens of keV) the story changes: photoelectric absorption grows steeply with atomic number, so lead becomes far better per gram. That is why the dentist's apron is lead (see Chapter 11).

Neutrons and cosmic rays: hydrogen wins

A neutron loses energy by bouncing off nuclei like a billiard ball. A head-on hit on a hydrogen nucleus (one proton, the same mass) can stop it dead; off lead, 207 times heavier, it keeps at least 98% of its energy, like a ping-pong ball off a bowling ball. So neutron shields are water, polyethylene (CH₂), or concrete, often with boron to absorb the slowed neutrons.

Cosmic rays add a second reason. A fast heavy nucleus hitting a heavy shield nucleus can shatter both into a spray of secondary neutrons, protons, and fragments, so a thin metal shield can make things worse. Hydrogen cannot shatter, light nuclei make fewer secondaries, and hydrogen has the most electrons per gram, so it slows charged particles best per kilogram launched. That is why NASA favors water and polyethylene.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Lead is the best radiation shield, period.

Reality: Lead is excellent against X-rays and compact against gamma rays, but poor against neutrons and it worsens cosmic-ray showers. The right shield depends on the radiation.

10.9Magnetic fields: steering charged particles

A magnetic field exerts a force only on a moving charge:

\[ \vec{F} = q\,\vec{v}\times\vec{B} \]

The force is perpendicular to both \(\vec v\) and \(\vec B\), so it changes the particle's direction but never its speed.

A force that always pushes sideways bends the path into a circle (Figure 10.7a). Setting \(qvB = mv^2/r\) gives the gyroradius:

\[ r = \frac{mv}{qB} \]

Any motion along the field stretches the circle into a helix around the field line (Figure 10.7b), so charged particles are guided along field lines, not across them. Photons and neutrons have \(q = 0\) and go straight.

Worked Example 10.4 — A 1 MeV proton in Earth's field

Given: A 1.0 MeV proton (\(m = 1.673\times10^{-27}\) kg) moving perpendicular to \(B = 30\ \mu\text{T}\), about Earth's field at the equator.

Find: Its speed and gyroradius.

Think: 1 MeV is far below the proton's 938 MeV rest energy, so \(KE = \tfrac12 mv^2\) is fine. Then use Eq. 10.7.

\[ v = \sqrt{\frac{2(1.0\times10^{6})(1.602\times10^{-19}\ \text{J})}{1.673\times10^{-27}\ \text{kg}}} = 1.38\times10^{7}\ \text{m/s} \]

\[ r = \frac{(1.673\times10^{-27})(1.38\times10^{7})}{(1.602\times10^{-19})(3.0\times10^{-5})} = 4.8\times10^{3}\ \text{m} \]

Answer: \(v \approx 1.4\times10^7\) m/s (about 5% of c), \(r \approx 4.8\) km, tiny compared with Earth's 6371 km radius. The proton is captured into spiraling along a field line instead of flying straight in.

You Asked

“How does a magnetic field help protect against radiation?”

A magnetic field deflects charged particles (protons, electrons, alpha particles, cosmic-ray nuclei) so they spiral along field lines instead of reaching the surface, but it does nothing at all to photons (UV, X-rays, gamma rays) or neutrons.

The Lorentz force \(\vec F = q\vec v\times\vec B\) is always sideways, so a charged particle curls into a helix around a field line (Figure 10.7). Earth's field turns most of the solar wind aside at the magnetopause and traps other particles in the Van Allen belts (Figure 10.8). With \(q = 0\), photons and neutrons feel no force. Earth stops those, and the most energetic cosmic rays, with its atmosphere: about 1 kg of air over every square centimeter, the mass of a 10 m column of water. Gases high up absorb the UV-C and X-rays.

Why not give spacecraft a magnetic shield? A 1 GeV cosmic-ray proton curls with a radius of about 6 m even in a 1 tesla field (30,000 times Earth's). Deflecting it means a strong field over meters around the crew: big superconducting magnets, cryogenics, heavy structure, and huge stored energy, and the highest-energy particles still get through. For now, shielding is mass.

Earth's magnetosphere, Van Allen belts, and aurora

Earth's field, about 25–65 µT at the surface, is roughly a dipole. The solar wind compresses it on the day side and stretches it into a long tail on the night side (Figure 10.8). Its outer boundary, the magnetopause, deflects most of the solar wind around Earth.

Field lines crowd together near the poles, and a spiraling particle heading into the stronger field is pushed back (a magnetic mirror). Trapped particles bounce pole to pole, forming two doughnut-shaped zones of intense radiation, the Van Allen belts: an inner belt (roughly 1,000–6,000 km up, mostly protons) and an outer belt (roughly 13,000–60,000 km, mostly electrons). The ISS, about 400 km up, stays below both except over the South Atlantic, where the inner belt dips low.

Near the magnetic poles, field lines dip into the atmosphere. Particles (mainly electrons) sliding down them excite oxygen and nitrogen 100–300 km up, which then emit light (Chapter 5): green 557.7 nm and red 630.0 nm from oxygen, blue-violet from nitrogen. That is the aurora.

10.10Space radiation and spacecraft shielding

Astronauts face galactic cosmic rays (steady, extremely energetic, hard to stop), solar particle events (sudden storms of lower-energy protons, easier to shield), and the trapped belts. The ISS crew gets about 0.2–0.9 mSv per day; Curiosity measured about 1.8 mSv per day on its way to Mars. Defenses:

  • hydrogen-rich mass around crew quarters (water, polyethylene, even food and supplies);
  • a heavily shielded "storm shelter" for solar events;
  • shorter trips, and timing missions around the solar cycle (an active Sun pushes some galactic cosmic rays away).
Rabbit Hole (optional) — Why a 1 GeV proton is so hard to steer

Safe to skip. Near light speed, use relativistic momentum: for 1 GeV of kinetic energy, \(pc = \sqrt{KE^2 + 2\,KE\,mc^2} = \sqrt{1 + 2(0.938)}\ \text{GeV} = 1.70\) GeV, so \(p = 9.1\times10^{-19}\) kg·m/s. Then \(r = p/(qB)\) is 5.7 m in 1 T and about 190 km in Earth's 30 µT. Earth's field works because it extends over thousands of kilometers; a spacecraft magnet must do the job within a few meters.

Chapter summary

  • Ionizing means about 10 eV or more per photon or particle (\(\lambda \lesssim 124\) nm). Brightness cannot substitute for photon energy.
  • UV-B is below the threshold but damages DNA by photochemistry (thymine dimers): sunburn.
  • Alpha: stopped by paper or skin. Beta: by millimeters of aluminum or plastic. Gamma/X-rays: reduced exponentially, best by dense mass. Neutrons: slowed by hydrogen.
  • DNA is damaged directly or by •OH radicals; double-strand and clustered breaks are the worst.
  • Gray = J/kg. Sievert = gray × biological weighting. The US average is about 6.2 mSv per year, half of it natural.
  • Inverse-square law: \(I \propto 1/r^2\). Attenuation: \(I = I_0 e^{-\mu x}\), HVL \(= 0.693/\mu\). For MeV gamma rays, mass per area is what counts.
  • Magnetic fields bend charged particles into helices (\(r = mv/qB\)) and do nothing to photons or neutrons. Earth relies on both its magnetosphere and its atmosphere (about 10 m of water equivalent).

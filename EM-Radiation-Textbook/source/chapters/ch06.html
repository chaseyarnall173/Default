Chapter 6

Reflection, Transmission, Absorption, and Refraction

A mirror, a window, a black T-shirt, and a diamond are all built from the same parts: nuclei and electrons. Which one reflects, which lets light through, which soaks it up, and which sparkles comes down to how those electrons move when a light wave shakes them.

The big picture

  • Light hitting matter is reflected, absorbed, or transmitted (some of it scattered on the way): R + A + T = 1.
  • One mechanism drives all of it: the wave's electric field shakes electrons, and shaken electrons radiate.
  • Reflection and refraction are coherent re-radiation, not "absorb, wait, re-emit."
  • In a material light keeps its frequency but its speed and wavelength drop by a factor n, which bends light at boundaries.

6.1The four fates of light

Light hitting an object can go back (reflected), through (transmitted), or into the material (absorbed). Scattering redirects some of it into many new directions; it counts as diffuse reflection or transmission (Figure 6.1).

\[ R + A + T = 1 \]

where \(R\), \(A\), and \(T\) are the fractions of the incoming energy reflected, absorbed, and transmitted (scattered light is included in \(R\) or \(T\)).

Absorbed energy usually becomes heat (Chapter 9). Inside an absorber, intensity falls off exponentially with depth \(x\):

\[ I = I_0\, e^{-\alpha x} \]

where \(I_0\) is the intensity just inside the surface and \(\alpha\) is the absorption coefficient (m\(^{-1}\)). After a depth of \(1/\alpha\), 37% remains.

Key Idea

R, A, and T depend on the material and the wavelength. Window glass transmits about 90% of visible light but almost none of the thermal infrared a warm object emits.

6.2What really happens: light shakes electrons

Light is an oscillating electric field (Chapter 1), so it pushes every charge in its path back and forth at the light's frequency. Nuclei are thousands of times heavier, so the electrons do nearly all the moving.

A shaken electron is an accelerating charge, and accelerating charges radiate. Each electron becomes a tiny antenna broadcasting at exactly the driving frequency. Every effect in this chapter is the sum of these small waves plus the original one (Figure 6.2a).

The response depends on how close the light's frequency is to the electron's natural frequencies. Far from them, the electron jiggles gently in step with the wave, and the light passes with only a slowdown. At a natural frequency the response is large and energy is absorbed (the gap-matching rule of Chapter 5).

Analogy

Push a swing at a random rhythm and it just wiggles along with your hand; push at its natural rhythm and you pour energy in. Off-resonance means transparent; on-resonance means absorption.

You Asked

“How does light work? What actually reflects the wave when it hits something?”

The electrons in the material do: the incoming wave shakes them, and the waves they radiate add up to the reflected wave (and, going forward, to the transmitted wave).

Deep inside a uniform material, the sideways and backward wavelets from neighboring electrons cancel. At a boundary, where the material suddenly begins, the backward wavelets have nothing to cancel against, so they add up to a reflected wave. Stronger electron response means stronger reflection: about 4% for glass, over 90% for metals, whose free electrons respond to every visible frequency (Section 6.4).

You Asked

“Is reflection the same thing as the material absorbing and re-emitting the EM wave?”

No. Reflection is coherent re-radiation by electrons that are driven by the wave; it is not a photon being absorbed into an excited state and re-emitted later. The two processes leave different fingerprints:

  • Driven re-radiation (reflection, transmission, refraction): works at any frequency; comes out at the same frequency; the electrons oscillate locked in phase with the wave; no excited state, so no waiting; direction fixed by geometry.
  • Absorption, then re-emission (fluorescence, Chapter 5): only when the photon energy matches a gap; the electron sits in a real excited state for nanoseconds; it emits at a random moment, in a random direction, usually at a longer wavelength.

The locked phase is what makes a mirror image possible. Billions of electrons re-radiate in step with the incoming wave, so their wavelets reinforce in one direction (the angle of reflection) and cancel in all others. If atoms re-emitted at random times in random directions, a mirror would merely glow, like a highlighter under a black light, with no image.

6.3Reflection: which way, and how much

Measured from the normal (the line perpendicular to the surface), the angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence: the one direction in which all the driven electrons' wavelets arrive in step.

\[ \theta_r = \theta_i \]

Bumps much smaller than the wavelength give specular (mirror-like) reflection. With bumps comparable to the wavelength or larger, every ray still obeys Equation 6.3 about its local normal, but the normals point every which way: diffuse reflection (Figure 6.3). White paper reflects most visible light, just diffusely.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: A mirror reflects light because it is very smooth.

Reality: Smoothness decides where reflected light goes; the electrons decide how much. Polished black glass is mirror-smooth but reflects about 4%. A mirror needs both a smooth surface and a metal coating.

At normal incidence, the reflected fraction depends only on the jump in refractive index \(n\) (Section 6.6) across the boundary:

\[ R = \left(\frac{n_2 - n_1}{n_2 + n_1}\right)^2 \]
Worked Example 6.1 — Seeing yourself in a window at night

Given: crown glass, \(n = 1.52\), in air; light at normal incidence.

Find: the reflectance of one surface, and the fraction transmitted by a single pane (2 surfaces) and a double-pane window (4 surfaces). Ignore absorption and repeated bounces.

Think: each air–glass surface is an index jump, so each one reflects according to Equation 6.4 and passes \(1 - R\).

\[ R = \left(\frac{1.52 - 1.00}{1.52 + 1.00}\right)^2 = (0.206)^2 = 0.043 \]

Single pane: \(T = (0.957)^2 = 0.92\). Double pane: \(T = (0.957)^4 = 0.84\).

Answer: about 4% per surface; a single pane transmits 92% and a double pane 84%. At night the 8% reflected from your bright room outshines the dim scene outside, so you see yourself.

6.4Why metals are shiny and opaque

In a metal, one to a few electrons per atom are free to roam (the conduction band, Chapter 5). A free electron has no spring holding it in place, so it has no natural frequency to be "far from." It responds strongly to every frequency below a cutoff called the plasma frequency, which for most metals lies in the ultraviolet.

The free electrons' re-radiated wave cancels the incoming wave within a thin surface layer, the skin depth (tens of nanometers for visible light), and sends nearly all the energy back out: metals reflect about 90–95% of visible light. The remainder is absorbed in that layer, so nothing gets through.

Materials Science Connection

The "sea of electrons" from metallic bonding explains electrical conductivity, thermal conductivity, and shine at once. Callister notes that visible light is absorbed within about 0.1 µm of a metal's surface, so only thinner films transmit. Gold leaf, about 0.1 µm thick, glows greenish when held up to a bright light.

Rabbit Hole (optional) — The plasma frequency

Safe to skip. Free electrons keep up with the field only below \(f_p = \frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{n_e e^2/(\varepsilon_0 m_e)}\), where \(n_e\) is the free-electron density. For aluminum (\(n_e \approx 1.8\times10^{29}\,\text{m}^{-3}\)) the matching photon energy is about 15 eV (80 nm, far UV), so aluminum mirrors work into the ultraviolet. Above \(f_p\) a metal turns transparent; X-rays pass through thin foils (Chapter 11). The ionosphere is a dilute electron "metal" with a plasma frequency in the radio range: AM and shortwave radio bounce off it, while FM and GPS pass through.

6.5Why glass is transparent (and where it isn't)

Glass is an insulator: pure silica has a band gap of roughly 9 eV, far above the 1.8–3.1 eV of visible photons. Visible light can only drive its tightly bound electrons off-resonance, so it is transmitted (and slowed), not absorbed. Glass is also uniform, with no pores or grain boundaries to scatter light. Outside the visible, things change (Table 6.1).

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Transparent materials let light slip through the gaps between their atoms.

Reality: Atoms are mostly empty space (Chapter 4), but a 500 nm wave is thousands of times wider than an atom and drives every electron it passes. Transparency means those electrons cannot absorb at that frequency and nothing scatters. Diamond (gap 5.5 eV) and graphite (no gap) are both pure carbon; one is clear, the other black.

6.6Refractive index: why light "slows down"

Inside a material, the forward wavelets from driven electrons add to the incoming wave. Each wavelet lags slightly in phase, so after every layer of atoms the combined crests sit a little farther back (Figure 6.2a) and advance more slowly than \(c\). The index of refraction \(n\) measures how much:

\[ n = \frac{c}{v} \]

where \(v\) is the speed of the wave crests (the phase velocity) in the material.

The electrons oscillate at the source's frequency, so frequency never changes. The wavelength shrinks instead:

\[ \lambda = \frac{\lambda_0}{n} \]

where \(\lambda_0\) is the vacuum wavelength. Photon energy \(hf\) is unchanged, so red light is still red underwater.

Key Idea

Crossing into a material changes light's speed and wavelength by the factor \(n\), never its frequency. Bending (refraction), dispersion, lenses, and fibers all follow from that one fact.

Myth vs. Reality

Myth: Light slows down in glass because atoms absorb it, hold it for a moment, and re-emit it.

Reality: Glass has no visible-frequency transition to absorb into, and absorb-and-re-emit would scramble direction and phase (Figure 6.2b), leaving glass cloudy. Between atoms, light travels at \(c\); the slower speed belongs to the combined crest pattern of incoming and re-radiated waves. Clincher: for X-rays, \(n\) is slightly less than 1, so crests move slightly faster than \(c\). No delay could do that; superposition does it naturally (energy and signals still never beat \(c\)).

Materials Science Connection

The index rises with electrons per volume and with how easily they shift (electronic polarizability). Same SiO₂, denser packing: crystalline quartz (2.65 g/cm³) has \(n \approx 1.54\); fused silica glass (2.20 g/cm³) has 1.458. Large, easily polarized ions such as Pb²⁺ and Ba²⁺ raise \(n\): leaded "flint" glasses reach about 1.7. Non-cubic crystals (quartz, calcite, sapphire) also have direction-dependent \(n\), called birefringence; cubic crystals and glasses do not (see Section 6.11).

6.7Snell's law: bending at a boundary

When a wave enters glass at an angle, one end of each wavefront reaches the slow medium first and lags. To keep crests continuous across the surface, the wavefront pivots and the ray bends (Figure 6.4):

\[ n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2 \]

with both angles measured from the normal. Entering a higher-\(n\) material, light bends toward the normal; entering a lower-\(n\) material, it bends away.

Analogy

A marching band crosses from pavement onto mud at an angle. The marchers who reach the mud first slow down, and the whole line swings around toward the normal.

Worked Example 6.2 — A red laser enters a glass block

Given: \(\lambda_0 = 633\) nm, \(\theta_1 = 45.0^\circ\) in air, glass \(n = 1.52\).

Find: the refraction angle, and the speed and wavelength inside the glass.

Think: Snell's law gives the angle; Equations 6.5 and 6.6 give speed and wavelength.

\[ \sin\theta_2 = \frac{1.00 \times \sin 45.0^\circ}{1.52} = 0.465 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_2 = 27.7^\circ \]

\[ v = \frac{3.00\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s}}{1.52} = 1.97\times10^{8}\,\text{m/s}, \qquad \lambda = \frac{633\,\text{nm}}{1.52} = 416\,\text{nm} \]

Answer: 27.7° from the normal; \(1.97\times10^{8}\) m/s; 416 nm. The beam is still red: its frequency, \(4.74\times10^{14}\) Hz, is unchanged.

6.8Total internal reflection and optical fibers

Going from high \(n\) to low \(n\), light bends away from the normal. At the critical angle \(\theta_c\) the refracted ray grazes the surface; beyond it, no light escapes and 100% reflects. This is total internal reflection (Figure 6.5a):

\[ \sin\theta_c = \frac{n_2}{n_1} \qquad (n_1 \gt n_2) \]

Nothing is absorbed, so total internal reflection beats any metal mirror. Diamond's small critical angle (24.4°) traps light for many bounces, which is why diamonds sparkle; binocular prisms use the same trick.

An optical fiber is a glass core inside cladding of slightly lower index; light bounces along the core by total internal reflection. Good fibers lose only about 0.2 dB per kilometer at 1550 nm, so light travels about 15 km before losing half its power.

Worked Example 6.3 — How steeply can light travel in a fiber?

Given: core \(n_1 = 1.48\), cladding \(n_2 = 1.46\).

Find: the critical angle at the core wall, and the largest angle from the axis at which light can enter the fiber end from air.

Think: rays must hit the wall beyond \(\theta_c\), so inside the core they travel within \(90^\circ - \theta_c\) of the axis. Snell's law at the flat end converts that to an angle in air.

\[ \theta_c = \sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac{1.46}{1.48}\right) = 80.6^\circ \quad\Rightarrow\quad 90^\circ - 80.6^\circ = 9.4^\circ \text{ from the axis} \]

\[ \sin\theta_\text{air} = 1.48 \sin 9.4^\circ = 0.242 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_\text{air} = 14.0^\circ \]

Answer: \(\theta_c = 80.6^\circ\); light entering within about 14° of the axis is trapped.

6.9Dispersion: prisms and rainbows

The index depends slightly on wavelength: dispersion. In glass and water, \(n\) is larger for violet than red, because violet's frequency is closer to the electrons' ultraviolet resonances. Crown glass (BK7) has \(n = 1.522\) at 486 nm (blue) and 1.514 at 656 nm (red); water goes from about 1.343 at 400 nm to 1.331 at 700 nm.

So a prism bends each color by a different angle (Figure 6.6a). In a rainbow, sunlight refracts into each raindrop, reflects once off the back, and refracts out, piling up near 42° from the antisolar point (the shadow of your head) for red and about 41° for violet. Red reaches you from slightly higher drops, so it forms the outer edge of the bow.

6.10Lenses and focusing

A lens is shaped so that refraction steers rays to a common point. A converging lens (thicker in the middle) bends parallel rays through the focal point F, a distance \(f\) (the focal length) away. A diverging lens (thinner in the middle) spreads them as if from a point behind it (Figure 6.7). For a thin lens:

\[ \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{d_o} + \frac{1}{d_i} \]

where \(d_o\) is the object distance and \(d_i\) the image distance (\(f \gt 0\) for converging lenses). Eyeglass strength is quoted as power \(P = 1/f\) in diopters (m\(^{-1}\)).

Your eye has about 60 diopters of power. About 43 (two-thirds) come from the cornea, because the biggest index jump is from air into the cornea; the lens adds an adjustable 15–20 diopters by changing shape.

6.11Opacity and translucency in ceramics and polymers

A transparent material can still look white and opaque. Every internal boundary where \(n\) changes reflects a little light (Equation 6.4) and redirects it; with enough boundaries, most light comes back out diffusely. That is why snow and frosted glass look white though ice and glass are clear.

In ceramics the main scatterers are (Figure 6.8):

  • Pores: a jump from \(n \approx 1.77\) to 1.00 scatters strongly; a few percent porosity makes a ceramic opaque.
  • Grain boundaries in non-cubic crystals: neighboring grains are oriented differently, so a birefringent material presents an index mismatch at every boundary.
  • Second-phase particles with a different \(n\), added on purpose to glazes as opacifiers.
Materials Science Connection

Sapphire vs. alumina. Both are Al₂O₃ in the corundum structure (O²⁻ in a hexagonal close-packed array, Al³⁺ in two-thirds of the octahedral sites). A single crystal (sapphire) is a transparent window for watches and barcode scanners. Fully dense polycrystalline alumina is translucent (GE's Lucalox sodium-lamp tubes). With about 5% porosity it is opaque white, like a spark-plug insulator. Cubic spinel (MgAl₂O₄) has no birefringence, so fully dense polycrystalline spinel can be transparent.

Polymers. Amorphous polymers such as acrylic (PMMA) and polycarbonate are clear. Semicrystalline HDPE (milk jugs) is cloudy: its denser crystalline regions have a higher \(n\) than the amorphous ones. PET bottles stay clear because processing keeps their crystals smaller than the wavelength of light.

Chapter summary

  • Light meeting matter is reflected, absorbed, or transmitted (some scattered): \(R + A + T = 1\), all wavelength-dependent.
  • The wave drives electrons, which re-radiate at the same frequency and in fixed phase. Reflection and refraction are this coherent re-radiation, not absorb-and-re-emit (fluorescence), which is delayed, random in direction, and usually red-shifted.
  • Roughness compared with the wavelength sets specular vs. diffuse reflection; \(R = [(n_2-n_1)/(n_2+n_1)]^2\) gives about 4% per glass surface.
  • Metals' free electrons respond below the plasma frequency: they reflect 90–95% and absorb the rest within about 0.1 µm. Glass passes visible light (gap far above 3.1 eV) but absorbs UVB and thermal IR.
  • \(n = c/v\); frequency is unchanged and wavelength becomes \(\lambda_0/n\). Snell's law gives the bending; beyond \(\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1\), total internal reflection traps light in fibers.
  • Dispersion separates colors in prisms and rainbows; lenses focus by refraction. Pores and birefringent grain boundaries scatter light, making ceramics translucent or opaque.
